# MCAT Psych/Soc — Complete Term Sheet

**What this is.** Every term AAMC's P/S content outline names, organized by AAMC's own foundational concept and content category, one line each, written so you can *recognize* it under 95 seconds — not recite it.

**How to read it.**
- `★` = this term carries a classic MCAT trap. If you only have 20 minutes, read the ★ lines and the `Split:` lines.
- `Split [XN]:` = the single feature that separates the two (or three) terms directly above it. `XN` is the pair ID in `research/06-psychsoc-items.md` §5.1 if you want the full trap note.
- Confusable terms are **adjacent by construction**. AAMC builds P/S option sets by taking the key and offering its nearest taxonomic neighbours (rules R1–R5, `06` §5.0). If you can state the axis, you don't need to recognize the scenario.

**Order is deliberate.** Sociology first and in most depth: it is 30% of the section (~18 questions), it is a closed vocabulary list with almost no mechanism, and it is where the cheapest points are. Psychology second (65%, ~38 q). Methods/stats and neuro-in-P/S last — each is a separate closed deck worth ~6 questions.

**Section shape:** 59 questions · 95 min · 10 passages (44 q) + 15 discretes.
**FC weights:** FC6 25% · FC7 35% · FC8 20% · FC9 15% · FC10 5%.

## 1. 9A — Theoretical Approaches
*The seven-item closed list. AAMC's Skill 2 item-type list names a "which sociological theory is consistent with this diagram" item explicitly. Memorize as a set, not as seven separate cards.*

- ★ **Functionalism (structural functionalism)** — society is a system of interdependent parts, each serving a function that keeps the whole stable.
- ★ **Conflict theory** — society is competition between groups over scarce resources; inequality persists because it serves those with power.
- ★ **Symbolic interactionism** — society is built out of face-to-face interaction and the shared meanings people negotiate through symbols.
- ★ **Social constructionism** — categories treated as natural (race, gender, illness, money) are real only because society collectively agrees to treat them as real.
- ★ **Exchange–rational choice theory** — people act to maximize reward and minimize cost; structure is the aggregate of those calculations.
- ★ **Feminist theory** — conflict theory with gender as the axis: how social structure produces and sustains gendered power.
  *Split [J1]: function/equilibrium · competition over resources · meaning-making in interaction · a category being defined as real · cost–benefit language (incentive, payoff, reward) · gender as the axis of power. The tell for social constructionism in a stem is "is not inherent but is defined by."*
  *Split [J6]: conflict vs feminist = same machine, axis swapped (class vs gender).*
  *Split [J5]: symbolic interactionism vs social constructionism = is the item about how meaning is negotiated in an interaction, or about how a category came to be treated as real?*
- ★ **Microsociology** — study of small-scale, face-to-face interaction.
- ★ **Macrosociology** — study of large-scale structure, institutions, and whole societies.
  *Split [J2]: symbolic interactionism (and usually exchange) are micro; functionalism, conflict, and feminist are macro. Sorting micro/macro first cuts the seven-item set in half and is usually decisive.*
- **Manifest function** — the intended, recognized consequence of a social structure or practice.
- **Latent function** — an unintended, unrecognized *beneficial* consequence.
- **Latent dysfunction** — an unintended, unrecognized *harmful* consequence.
  *Split [J3/J4]: first ask intended vs not; then beneficial vs harmful. School's manifest function is education; latent function is childcare and mate-finding; latent dysfunction is reproducing class inequality.*
- **Social fact** — a norm, value, or structure that exists outside the individual and constrains behavior (Durkheim's unit of analysis).
- **Mechanical solidarity** — cohesion in simple societies arising from shared values and similar work.
- **Organic solidarity** — cohesion in complex societies arising from interdependence under a specialized division of labor.
  *Split [J24]: similarity vs interdependence. Lives under the outline's "division of labor."*
- **Division of labor** — the specialization of tasks across a society or organization; more specialization → organic solidarity and greater interdependence.
- **Social solidarity** — the degree to which a society's members are integrated and bound to each other.

**Who owns what (retrieval handles only — AAMC never asks "who proposed X"):** Durkheim = functionalism, anomie, division of labor, solidarity. Marx = conflict theory, class/false consciousness. Weber = authority types, bureaucracy, power/prestige/party, verstehen. Mead + Goffman + Cooley = symbolic interactionism. Merton = strain theory, manifest/latent function, reference group, self-fulfilling prophecy, role conflict/strain. Bourdieu = cultural and social capital, social reproduction.

## 2. 9A — Social Institutions
*Five institutions on the outline: education, family, religion, government/economy, health & medicine. Health & medicine is over-tested relative to its size — the section exists to train physicians.*

### 2.1 Education
- ★ **Hidden curriculum** — the unstated norms, values, and behaviors schools transmit alongside academic content (punctuality, deference to authority, competition).
- ★ **Teacher expectancy (Pygmalion effect)** — a teacher's belief about a student causes that student's performance to move toward the belief.
  *Split [J22]: hidden curriculum is what the *institution* transmits to everyone; teacher expectancy is what one *person's belief* does to one student. Teacher expectancy is a self-fulfilling prophecy wearing an institutional uniform — cross-reference [I9].*
- **Educational segregation** — separation of groups into different schools or tracks.
- **Educational stratification** — unequal educational outcomes ranked by group position.
  *Split [J23]: separation vs ranked inequality of outcome. The outline pairs them in one bullet, so they get offered together.*
- **Tracking** — sorting students into ability-based streams; a mechanism of educational stratification.

### 2.2 Family and kinship
- **Kinship** — social ties based on descent, marriage, or adoption.
- **Household** — people sharing a residence, regardless of relation.
- **Family** — the socially recognized unit of kinship and care.
  *Split [J25]: descent/marriage tie · co-residence · social recognition. A roommate is a household, not kin; a cousin abroad is kin, not household.*
- **Nuclear family** — parents and their children only.
- **Extended family** — kin beyond the nuclear unit sharing residence or obligation.
- **Monogamy / polygamy (polygyny, polyandry)** — one spouse / multiple spouses (multiple wives, multiple husbands).
- **Endogamy** — marriage within one's own social group.
- **Exogamy** — marriage outside one's own social group.
- **Patrilineal / matrilineal / bilateral descent** — kinship traced through the father's line / the mother's line / both.
- **Patrilocal / matrilocal / neolocal residence** — couple lives near the husband's kin / the wife's kin / independently.
- **Family violence** — child abuse, elder abuse, and spousal/intimate-partner abuse; on the outline as a named subtopic.

### 2.3 Religion
- **Religiosity** — the depth and intensity of a person's religious belief and practice.
- **Religious affiliation** — nominal membership in a religious body.
  *Split [J15]: intensity vs membership. You can be affiliated with near-zero religiosity.*
- ★ **Church** — large, established religious organization well integrated with the surrounding society (low tension).
- ★ **Sect** — a breakaway group in higher tension with mainstream society, usually splintered from an established church.
- ★ **Cult (new religious movement)** — novel, charismatically led, in the highest tension with society; not derived from an existing body.
  *Split [J13]: tension with society, low → high, plus origin (established · breakaway · novel).*
- **Denomination** — a church that coexists with other churches rather than claiming exclusive legitimacy.
- ★ **Secularization** — the declining social authority of religion as societies modernize.
- ★ **Fundamentalism** — the reassertion of literal, traditional religious belief, often in reaction to modernization.
- ★ **Modernization** — a society's structural shift toward industrial, rational, bureaucratic forms.
  *Split [J14]: religion loses authority · religion reasserts itself · society industrializes. AAMC lists exactly these three under "religion and social change," so all three get offered together.*

### 2.4 Government and economy
- ★ **Power** — the ability to impose your will on others, with or without their consent.
- ★ **Authority** — power that is regarded as *legitimate* by those subject to it.
  *Split [J16]: legitimacy. A mugger has power; a police officer has authority.*
- **Traditional authority** — legitimacy from long-standing custom and inherited position (monarchy).
- **Charismatic authority** — legitimacy from an individual's extraordinary personal qualities.
- **Rational-legal authority** — legitimacy from written rules and the office rather than the person.
  *Split [J17]: custom · person · rules. Weber's three types; an R2 set — expect all three offered.*
- **Democracy / authoritarianism / totalitarianism / monarchy** — rule by the people / by a small unaccountable group / by a regime controlling public *and* private life / by hereditary sovereign.
- **Capitalism** — private ownership of the means of production, market allocation, profit motive.
- **Socialism** — collective or state ownership of production, allocation by plan or need.
- **Welfare state** — a capitalist economy with state provision of social insurance and services.
- **Division of labor (economic)** — specialization of productive tasks; drives both efficiency and interdependence.

### 2.5 Health and medicine
- ★ **Medicalization** — the process by which a non-medical condition or behavior comes to be defined and treated as a medical problem (shyness → social anxiety disorder; childbirth → hospital procedure).
- **Demedicalization** — the reverse: a condition ceases to be treated as medical (homosexuality removed from the DSM).
  *Split [J18]: direction. Both are processes, not states.*
- ★ **Sick role (Parsons)** — the socially granted rights and duties of an ill person: exempt from normal roles and not blamed for the condition, but obliged to want to get well and to seek competent help.
- ★ **Illness experience** — the ill person's own subjective, lived experience of being sick, including identity and daily disruption.
  *Split [J19/J20]: medicalization = society defines the behavior as illness. Sick role = society's normative expectations *of the patient*. Illness experience = the patient's own interior view. Adjacent bullets on the outline, so pure R1 distractors for each other.*
- ★ **Social epidemiology** — the study of how health and disease are distributed across social positions (class, race, gender, neighborhood).
  *Split [J21]: social epidemiology asks how *social position* patterns disease; clinical epidemiology asks how disease behaves in clinical populations.*
- **Delivery of health care** — how care is financed, organized, and accessed; the outline names it as its own subtopic (insurance model, access barriers, provider distribution).
- **Illness / disease / sickness** — the subjective experience / the biological pathology / the social role and recognition.

## 3. 9A — Culture
*Almost every leaf here is a named outline bullet, and several are named as explicit pairs. High density of guaranteed points per minute of study.*

- **Culture** — the shared beliefs, language, rituals, symbols, values, and objects of a group, transmitted across generations.
- **Elements of culture** — beliefs, language, rituals, symbols, values, norms (the outline's own enumeration).
- ★ **Material culture** — the physical objects a society makes and uses (tools, buildings, clothing, technology).
- ★ **Symbolic (nonmaterial) culture** — the ideas of a society: beliefs, values, norms, language, symbols.
  *Split [J7]: can you touch it? Named as an explicit pair on the outline.*
- ★ **Culture lag** — nonmaterial culture (norms, laws, ethics) failing to keep pace with change in material culture/technology.
- ★ **Culture shock** — the disorientation a person feels on entering an unfamiliar culture.
  *Split [J8]: lag is a property of a *society over time*; shock is an experience of an *individual crossing a boundary*. Adjacent outline bullets, so a pure R1 distractor pair — and one of the most frequently confused in 9A.*
- ★ **Assimilation** — a minority group is absorbed into the dominant culture and loses its distinct identity.
- ★ **Multiculturalism (pluralism)** — distinct cultures are preserved and given equal standing side by side.
  *Split [J10]: absorption vs preservation. Adjacent outline bullets.*
- **Acculturation** — adopting some traits of a new culture while retaining one's original identity.
- **Amalgamation (melting pot)** — two or more cultures blend into a genuinely new one.
  *Split [J11]: assimilation = A becomes B. Acculturation = A adds some B, stays A. Amalgamation = A + B → C.*
- ★ **Piagetian assimilation vs sociological assimilation** — fitting new information into an existing cognitive schema vs a minority group absorbing into a dominant culture.
  *Split [C12]: a true cross-domain homonym; both are outline terms (6B and 9A) and an option set can mix them. Check whether the stem is about a *child's thinking* or a *group's culture*.*
- ★ **Subculture** — a group with distinct norms and values that still operates inside the dominant culture (surgeons, gamers, a regional cuisine).
- ★ **Counterculture** — a group whose norms and values actively *oppose* the dominant culture.
  *Split [J9]: distinct vs oppositional. Named as a pair on the outline.*
- ★ **Cultural transmission** — culture passed *within* a group across generations (parent → child, school → student).
- ★ **Cultural diffusion** — culture spreading *between* groups or societies (trade, migration, media).
  *Split [J12]: vertical within-group vs horizontal between-group. Named as a pair on the outline.*
- **Cultural universal** — a practice found in every known culture (family, language, some form of ritual).
- **Cultural relativism** — judging a culture by its own standards rather than your own.
- **Ethnocentrism** — judging another culture by the standards of your own, usually treating yours as superior.
  *Split [I11]: named as an explicit pair on the outline ("Ethnocentrism vs. cultural relativism"). One of the safest guaranteed points on the section.*
- **Xenocentrism** — valuing another culture over your own.
- **Mass media** — the institutions that transmit information and culture to large, dispersed audiences.
- **Popular culture** — the widely shared, commercially transmitted culture of a mass society; contrast **high culture**, the culture of elites.
- **Norm** — a shared rule about how one ought to behave.
- **Value** — a shared standard of what is good or desirable (values generate norms).
- **Symbol** — anything to which people assign shared meaning; language is the central symbolic system.
- **Ritual** — a formalized, repeated symbolic practice that expresses and reinforces shared meaning.
- **Sapir–Whorf / linguistic relativity** — the structure of a language shapes what and how its speakers think.
- **Evolution and human culture** — the outline's PSY/BIO leaf: culture as an adaptive, transmissible system that shapes and is shaped by biological evolution.

## 4. 9B — Demographic Characteristics
*The five demographic variables AAMC names: age, gender, race/ethnicity, immigration status, sexual orientation. Every one of them is framed on the outline as socially constructed — that framing is itself the keyed answer more often than not.*

### 4.1 Age
- ★ **Age cohort** — a group of people born in the same interval, moving through the life course together.
- **Generation** — an age cohort that has acquired a shared cultural identity and label.
- **Period effect** — an influence that hits every age group at the same historical moment (a pandemic, a recession).
- **Cohort effect** — an influence specific to people born at a particular time, carried with them through life.
- **Age effect** — a change that comes from being a certain age, regardless of when you were born.
  *Split [K12]: birth interval · shared identity · everyone at once · one birth group across life · aging itself. In a cross-sectional study, age and cohort effects are inseparable — that is the classic confound.*
- **Life course perspective** — aging as a socially patterned sequence of roles and transitions, not just a biological process.
- **Ageism** — prejudice or discrimination based on age.
- **Social significance of aging** — the outline's leaf: how societies assign status, roles, and resources by age; includes dependency ratio and retirement as a social institution.
- **Dependency ratio** — the ratio of non-working-age people (young + old) to working-age people; rises as a population ages.

### 4.2 Gender and sexual orientation
- ★ **Sex** — biological and anatomical classification (chromosomes, gonads, genitalia).
- ★ **Gender** — the socially constructed roles, behaviors, and expectations attached to sex.
  *Split [K1]: biology vs social construction. Named as an explicit pair on the outline. A stem that says "expectations about how a person should behave" is gender, always.*
- **Gender identity** — a person's internal sense of their own gender.
- **Sexual orientation** — the pattern of a person's romantic or sexual attraction.
  *Split [K2]: who you are vs who you are attracted to. Listed as two separate demographic variables on the outline.*
- **Social construction of gender** — the process by which gender differences are produced and maintained by socialization, institutions, and interaction rather than by biology.
- **Doing gender** — treating gender as something continuously performed in interaction rather than possessed.
- ★ **Gender segregation** — the sorting of men and women into different roles, occupations, or spaces (occupational sex segregation).
- **Gender stratification** — unequal distribution of power, prestige, and resources by gender.
- **Glass ceiling** — an invisible barrier blocking women (or minorities) from top positions.
- **Sexism** — prejudice or discrimination based on sex or gender.

### 4.3 Race, ethnicity, immigration
- ★ **Race** — a socially constructed category based on *perceived* physical traits, with no consistent biological basis.
- ★ **Ethnicity** — a socially constructed category based on shared cultural heritage, ancestry, language, or religion.
  *Split [K4]: perceived body vs shared culture. Both are constructed — the difference is which raw material society builds on.*
- ★ **Racialization** — the process of assigning racial meaning to a group or practice that previously had none.
- ★ **Racial formation** — the ongoing sociohistorical process by which racial categories themselves are created, inhabited, transformed, and destroyed.
  *Split [K3]: racialization acts on a *group*; racial formation acts on the *category system*. Adjacent outline bullets, so an R1 distractor pair by construction.*
- **Symbolic ethnicity** — an optional, occasional ethnic identity retained by well-assimilated groups.
- **Minority group** — a group subordinate in power and access, regardless of numerical size.
- **Immigration** — movement *into* a country.
- **Emigration** — movement *out of* a country.
  *Split [K8]: same physical movement described from opposite ends. An R4 trap — read which country the stem is standing in.*
- **Net migration** — immigration minus emigration for a population over a period.
- **Immigration status** — the legal category assigned to a migrant, which conditions access to work, care, and rights; the outline names its intersection with race and ethnicity.
- **Patterns of immigration** — chain migration, labor migration, refugee/asylum flows, return migration.

## 5. 9B — Demographic Shifts and Social Change
*Contains the section's only real figure-reading sociology item (population pyramids — AAMC names it explicitly in its Skill 2 item-type list).*

### 5.1 Theories of demographic change
- ★ **Malthusian theory** — population grows geometrically while food supply grows arithmetically, so growth is checked catastrophically by famine, disease, and war.
- ★ **Demographic transition theory** — as a society industrializes, mortality falls first and fertility falls later, producing a burst of growth then stabilization at low birth and low death rates.
  *Split [K9]: catastrophe vs orderly four-stage transition. Named as the outline's only two examples of "theories of demographic change" — so they are each other's distractor, and demographic transition is the keyed answer more often.*
- **Demographic transition stage 1** — high birth rate, high death rate → population stable and small (pre-industrial).
- **Demographic transition stage 2** — death rate falls, birth rate stays high → rapid growth.
- **Demographic transition stage 3** — birth rate falls → growth slows.
- **Demographic transition stage 4** — low birth, low death → stable, older population; **stage 5** (sometimes given) is birth below death → decline.
- ★ **Population pyramid** — an age-sex histogram whose shape encodes the population's growth stage.
- ★ **Expansive pyramid** — wide base, narrow top: high fertility, rapid growth, young population (stage 2).
- ★ **Constrictive pyramid** — narrower base than middle: falling fertility, aging population, eventual decline.
- ★ **Stationary pyramid** — roughly rectangular sides: stable, low fertility and low mortality (stage 4).
  *Split [K10/K11]: read the *base width relative to the middle*. Wide = expanding; pinched = constricting; straight = stationary. AAMC names a population-pyramid item explicitly.*

### 5.2 Fertility, mortality, migration
- ★ **Crude birth rate** — births per 1,000 people in the total population per year.
- ★ **Crude death rate** — deaths per 1,000 people in the total population per year.
- ★ **Total fertility rate** — the average number of children a woman would bear over her lifetime at current rates.
- ★ **Age-specific rate** — a birth or death rate calculated within a defined age band only.
  *Split [K5]: "crude" = denominator is the whole population, so it is confounded by age structure. "Total" = per woman over a lifetime. "Age-specific" = within one band, which is why it is the honest comparison across populations with different age distributions.*
- **Fecundity** — the biological *capacity* to reproduce (an upper bound); **fertility** is actual reproduction.
  *Split [K6]: capacity vs realization. Fecundity is always higher than fertility.*
- **Mortality rate** — deaths per unit population; **morbidity** is the rate of disease, not death.
- **Infant mortality rate** — deaths under age 1 per 1,000 live births; the standard proxy for a population's overall health and health-system quality.
- **Life expectancy** — the average number of years a newborn is expected to live under current conditions.
- **Replacement fertility** — the TFR (~2.1) at which a population exactly replaces itself.
- ★ **Push factors** — conditions driving people *out* of an origin (war, famine, unemployment, persecution).
- ★ **Pull factors** — conditions attracting people *to* a destination (jobs, safety, family, political freedom).
  *Split [K7]: which end of the move is the stem describing? Named as an explicit pair on the outline.*

### 5.3 Social movements
- ★ **Social movement** — an organized, sustained, goal-directed collective effort to promote or resist social change.
- ★ **Collective behavior** — spontaneous, unstructured group action: fads, crazes, mass hysteria, riots, panics.
  *Split [K18]: organization and duration. Movements are 9B; collective behavior is 7B — a cross-category R1 trap.*
- ★ **Relative deprivation** — the perception of being worse off *relative to a reference group*, which is what actually motivates people to join movements.
  *Split [K13]: this is a *perception*, not a poverty measure — do not confuse it with relative poverty [L8] or absolute deprivation. AAMC files it under social movements, not under inequality, and that placement is the clue to how it will be tested. Movements arise from perceived gaps, not from the worst absolute conditions.*
- **Resource mobilization theory** — movements succeed when they can command resources (money, members, media, leadership), not merely when grievance is high.
- **Mass society theory** — movements attract socially isolated people seeking belonging.
- **Types of movements** — reform (change part of the system), revolutionary (replace the system), reactionary (return to a prior state), alternative/redemptive (change individuals).
- **Movement stages** — emergence → coalescence → bureaucratization → decline (success, failure, co-optation, repression, or going mainstream).
- **Movement strategies and tactics** — the outline's leaf: protest, boycott, litigation, lobbying, civil disobedience, framing.
- **Proactive vs reactive movement** — pushing for a new arrangement vs resisting a change already underway.

### 5.4 Globalization and urbanization
- **Globalization** — increasing worldwide interdependence of economies, communication, culture, and populations.
- **Modernization** — a single society's structural shift toward industrial, rational, bureaucratic forms.
  *Split [K17]: between societies vs within one. Globalization is 9B; modernization appears in 9A under religion and social change.*
- **Factors contributing to globalization** — communication technology, transportation, economic interdependence, transnational institutions.
- **Social changes in globalization** — the outline names civil unrest and terrorism explicitly, along with cultural diffusion and labor migration.
- **Urbanization** — a rising proportion of a population living in cities; driven by industrialization.
- **Suburbanization** — population and capital moving from the urban core to the periphery.
  *Split [K14]: into cities vs out to the fringe. Adjacent outline bullets.*
- **Urban decline** — disinvestment, population loss, and infrastructure decay in a city core.
- **White flight** — the specific racialized mechanism in which white residents leave neighborhoods as minority residents arrive.
  *Split [K16]: general disinvestment vs a named racialized mechanism.*
- ★ **Gentrification** — affluent newcomers move into a lower-income urban neighborhood, raising costs and displacing existing residents.
- ★ **Urban renewal** — planned, often state-led redevelopment of an urban area, frequently by clearance.
  *Split [K15]: market-driven in-migration by individuals vs a deliberate top-down program. Named as an explicit pair on the outline; the displacement can look identical, so read for *who is acting*.*
- **Urban sprawl** — low-density, car-dependent outward expansion of a metropolitan area.
- **Megalopolis / metropolis** — a chain of adjoining metropolitan areas / a large city and its surrounding region.

## 6. 10A — Social Inequality
*One content category, 5% of the section (~3 questions), and roughly 30 terms total. The single highest points-per-term block on the entire MCAT. AAMC's own published Skill 2 sample item is a cultural-capital item.*

### 6.1 Spatial inequality
- ★ **Residential segregation** — the spatial separation of groups (usually by race or class) into different neighborhoods.
- ★ **Environmental justice** — the principle that no group should bear a disproportionate share of environmental health risks; as a *finding*, it names the observation that poor and minority communities are sited nearer to hazards.
  *Split [L16]: who lives apart vs who is exposed. Adjacent bullets under Spatial Inequality — an easy R1 pair.*
- **Neighborhood safety and violence** — the outline's leaf: neighborhood-level exposure to violence as an independent determinant of health, separate from individual income.
- **Redlining** — the historical practice of denying loans and insurance by neighborhood, a named mechanism of residential segregation.
- **Food desert** — an area with little access to affordable fresh food; a spatial-inequality mechanism for diet-related disease.
- **Global inequality** — inequality *between* countries.
- **Domestic stratification** — inequality *within* one country.
  *Split [L18]: which boundary is the comparison drawn across? The outline names "global inequalities" explicitly.*

### 6.2 Social class and capital
- **Social stratification** — the systematic ranking of groups in a hierarchy of access to resources.
- **Social class** — a position in a structural system of stratification, defined by relation to resources and production.
- **Socioeconomic status (SES)** — a composite index of income, education, and occupation used to locate an individual.
  *Split [L11]: SES is a *measurement* of an individual; class is a *structural position*. The outline pairs them in one bullet.*
- ★ **Cultural capital** — non-financial assets — tastes, manners, credentials, cultural knowledge, "how to act in this room" — that confer advantage.
- ★ **Social capital** — the value embedded in your *network of relationships*: who you know and what they will do for you.
  *Split [L1]: what you know how to do and how to seem vs who you know. Named as an explicit pair on the outline, and AAMC's own published Skill 2 exemplar keys on cultural capital ("cultural distinctions associated with elite classes will be more valued"). **The single highest-value stratification pair on the section.***
- **Economic capital** — money and property.
- **Human capital** — skills, training, and education embodied in a person.
  *Split [L2]: cultural (knowing which fork) · social (knowing the host) · economic (owning the restaurant) · human (being able to cook).*
- ★ **Class consciousness** — awareness of one's true class position and shared interests with others in it.
- ★ **False consciousness** — holding beliefs that misrepresent your class position and work against your own interests.
  *Split [L3]: seeing your position accurately vs not. Named as an explicit pair on the outline; pure Marx.*
- ★ **Social reproduction** — the transmission of inequality across generations, so that class position reproduces itself.
- ★ **Meritocracy** — a system in which position is earned by ability and effort.
  *Split [L5]: inherited vs earned. Both named on the outline; meritocracy is the ideology social reproduction contradicts, so they are natural distractors for each other.*
  *Split [L4]: social reproduction vs social mobility — inequality staying put across generations vs individuals moving. Direct opposites, offered together.*
- ★ **Power** — the ability to impose your will.
- ★ **Prestige** — social honor, respect, and deference attached to a position.
- ★ **Privilege** — unearned advantage accruing from group membership.
  *Split [L10]: the outline lists "power, privilege, and prestige" as one bullet and repeats it in 8B. Weber's class (economic) / status (prestige) / party (power) sits underneath. A physician has high prestige and moderate power; a lobbyist has the reverse.*
- ★ **Intersectionality** — overlapping identities (race × gender × class × age) produce experiences that are *qualitatively distinct*, not simply the sum of separate disadvantages.
  *Split [L13]: the trap answer is always the additive framing ("she faces both racism and sexism"). The keyed answer is that the combination produces something neither alone produces.*
- **Caste system** — closed stratification: position ascribed at birth, mobility essentially impossible.
- **Class system** — open stratification: position partly achieved, mobility possible.
  *Split [L12]: open vs closed, ascribed vs achieved.*

### 6.3 Social mobility
- ★ **Intergenerational mobility** — a change in position *between* generations (parent → child).
- ★ **Intragenerational mobility** — a change in position *within* one person's own lifetime.
  *Split [L6]: "inter" = between generations; "intra" = inside one life. Named as a pair on the outline.*
- ★ **Vertical mobility** — a move that changes social rank (up or down).
- ★ **Horizontal mobility** — a move at the same rank (nurse → teacher).
  *Split [L7]: does the rank change? Named as a pair on the outline. Crosses with [L6] into a genuine 2×2 — AAMC builds exactly that option set, so drill all four cells, not the diagonal.*
- **Structural mobility** — mobility caused by changes in the economy itself (whole occupations appear or vanish), not by individual effort.
- **Exchange mobility** — individuals trade places while the overall distribution stays fixed.
- **Downward mobility** — a fall in rank; often the keyed answer where a stem describes deindustrialization or illness-driven job loss.

### 6.4 Poverty and exclusion
- ★ **Absolute poverty** — falling below a fixed subsistence threshold: unable to meet basic needs, defined identically everywhere.
- ★ **Relative poverty** — falling below the standard of living normal *in your own society*, however rich that society is.
  *Split [L8]: fixed threshold vs society-relative threshold. Named as a pair on the outline. And distinct again from **relative deprivation** [K13], which is a *perception* that drives social movements, not a measure of income.*
- ★ **Social exclusion** — being systematically blocked from participation in the economic, political, and social life of a society.
- **Social isolation** — a lack of social contact and relationships.
- **Segregation** — spatial or institutional separation of groups.
  *Split [L9]: the outline's Poverty bullet reads "Social exclusion (segregation and isolation)" — AAMC has bundled all three, so expect them offered together. Exclusion is the systemic process; isolation and segregation are its forms.*
- **Poverty line / threshold** — the administratively fixed income cutoff defining absolute poverty in a country.
- **Culture of poverty** — the (contested) claim that poverty persists because of transmitted values and behaviors rather than structure; typically appears as a *distractor* against structural explanations.
- **Feminization of poverty** — the rising share of the poor who are women and female-headed households.

### 6.5 Health and healthcare disparities
- ★ **Socioeconomic gradient in health** — health improves at *every* step up the SES ladder, not just above a poverty threshold.
  *Split [L14]: the gradient claim (continuous, all the way up) is the keyed answer; the threshold claim ("only the poorest are affected") is the standard distractor.*
- ★ **Health disparity** — a systematic difference in health *outcomes* between groups (mortality, disease prevalence, life expectancy).
- ★ **Healthcare disparity** — a systematic difference in *access to, use of, or quality of* medical care.
  *Split [L15]: outcome vs care. AAMC lists these as two separate topics on its 10A page, which makes them an R1 distractor pair by construction. "Black patients receive less pain medication" = healthcare disparity. "Black patients have higher hypertension rates" = health disparity.*
- ★ **Individual discrimination** — one person's unequal treatment of another.
- ★ **Institutional discrimination** — a policy or standard practice of an organization that produces unequal outcomes, with or without any intent.
- **Structural discrimination** — society-wide arrangements across many institutions that compound disadvantage.
  *Split [I2/L17]: an actor · an organization's rule · the whole arrangement. Individual vs institutional is named verbatim as a pair on the outline. The tell for institutional is that no individual in the vignette intends harm.*

## 7. 7B (SOC) — Norms, Deviance, Collective Behavior, Socialization
*The sociology half of content category 7B. Contains the highest-value sociology R2 set on the section: the three named perspectives on deviance.*

### 7.1 Norms and sanctions
- **Social norm** — a shared rule or expectation about how members of a group ought to behave.
- ★ **Folkways** — everyday customs and etiquette; violating them gets you thought rude (facing backward in an elevator).
- ★ **Mores** — norms with moral weight; violating them gets you thought immoral (adultery, cheating).
- ★ **Taboos** — prohibitions so strong that violation provokes revulsion and often criminal sanction (incest, cannibalism).
  *Split [H13]: strength of the reaction — rude · immoral · revolting. All three named verbatim on the outline, so expect all three offered.*
- **Sanction** — a reward or punishment applied to enforce a norm.
- ★ **Formal sanction** — officially administered by an institution (fine, prison, diploma, medal).
- ★ **Informal sanction** — socially administered by people (ridicule, gossip, applause, a look).
- ★ **Positive sanction** — a reward for conformity.
- ★ **Negative sanction** — a punishment for deviance.
  *Split [H14/H15]: two independent axes — formal/informal × positive/negative — which cross into a genuine 2×2. AAMC's R5 construction offers all four cells, so a promotion (formal positive), a fine (formal negative), praise (informal positive), and a snub (informal negative) are the four options.*
- ★ **Anomie** — a state of normlessness in a society, in which shared norms weaken or lose their grip on behavior (Durkheim; caused by rapid change).
- **Alienation** — a person's estrangement from their labor, their product, other people, and themselves (Marx).
  *Split [H16]: a property of *society* (norms have failed) vs a condition of the *individual under capitalism*. Different theorist, different mechanism, and they are each other's standard distractor.*
- **Social control** — the mechanisms by which a society regulates behavior and enforces conformity.
- **Formal vs informal social control** — law, police, courts vs socialization, shame, peer reaction.
  *Split [H21]: social control is *external* regulation; socialization is the *internalization* of norms so external control becomes unnecessary. Both named on the outline.*
- **Conformity** — adjusting behavior to match a group standard.
- **Deviance** — any violation of a social norm, from the trivial to the criminal; not inherently harmful, only norm-violating.

### 7.2 Perspectives on deviance
- ★ **Differential association theory** — deviance is *learned* through interaction in intimate groups, like any other behavior; you learn techniques and motives from the people around you.
- ★ **Labeling theory** — deviance is not in the act but in the *reaction*: an act becomes deviant when society successfully applies the label.
- ★ **Strain theory** — deviance arises when a society holds out cultural goals (wealth) but blocks legitimate means of reaching them for some groups.
  *Split [H18]: learned from whom · assigned by whom · caused by a structural gap. **All three named verbatim on the outline as "perspectives on deviance" — the single highest-value sociology R2 set. Expect all three in the same option list.***
  *Split [H17]: strain vs anomie — Merton's strain is the *gap between goals and means*; Durkheim's anomie is *normlessness*. Merton built strain out of anomie, and the trap is treating them as identical.*
- **Merton's five adaptations to strain** — conformity (accept goals + means), innovation (accept goals, reject means → crime), ritualism (reject goals, keep means), retreatism (reject both → withdrawal), rebellion (replace both).
- ★ **Primary deviance** — an initial norm violation that produces no change in self-concept.
- ★ **Secondary deviance** — deviance adopted as an identity *after* being labeled, which then produces more deviance.
  *Split [H19]: has the label been applied and internalized? This is labeling theory's internal machinery — and it is a self-fulfilling prophecy [I9] operating on a deviant identity.*
- **Social disorganization theory** — deviance is higher where community institutions and ties are weak, independent of who lives there.
- **Control theory (social bond)** — deviance occurs when a person's bonds to society (attachment, commitment, involvement, belief) are weak.
- **Stigma** — a discrediting attribute that spoils a person's social identity and reduces them in others' eyes.
  *Split [I13]: stigma is a *mark on a person* (SOC-tagged); a stereotype is a *generalized belief about a group* (PSY-tagged). The discipline tag tells you which lens the item wants.*

### 7.3 Collective behavior
- ★ **Fad** — a short-lived, intense enthusiasm for a novel practice or object.
- **Craze** — a fad people believe will be lasting or profitable.
- **Fashion** — a gradually changing, longer-lasting style preference.
- ★ **Mass hysteria** — a rapidly spreading false belief plus fear, sometimes with physical symptoms and no organic cause.
- ★ **Riot** — spontaneous, violent, relatively unorganized collective action.
- **Panic** — collective flight from a perceived threat.
- **Mob vs crowd** — an emotionally aroused group acting toward a specific target vs any temporary gathering.
- **Rumor / urban legend** — unverified information spread through informal channels; the transmission substrate for mass hysteria.
  *Split [H20]: fads, mass hysteria, and riots are named together on the outline as "aspects of collective behavior," so they are each other's distractors. All are *spontaneous and unorganized* — that is what separates the whole family from social movements [K18].*

### 7.4 Socialization
- **Socialization** — the lifelong process by which a person internalizes the norms, values, and roles of their society.
- ★ **Agents of socialization** — the outline's own four examples: **the family** (primary, earliest, most durable), **peers** (voluntary, dominant in adolescence), **mass media** (broad, indirect), **the workplace** (adult, role-specific).
  *Split [H22]: identify which agent the vignette has doing the work — items are usually written as a single-agent attribution.*
- **Primary socialization** — childhood acquisition of basic norms, language, and self.
- **Secondary socialization** — later learning of the norms of a specific setting or subgroup.
- **Anticipatory socialization** — rehearsing the norms of a role you expect to occupy (a pre-med acting like a physician).
- **Resocialization** — deliberately discarding old norms and acquiring new, often opposed ones (boot camp, prison, rehab).
- **Total institution** — an isolating setting that controls all aspects of life and resocializes its inhabitants; *not on AAMC's outline* — low priority, and the outline-safe answer is "resocialization."
- **Peer pressure** — explicit or implicit influence exerted by peers on a person's behavior.
  *Split [H23]: peer pressure names the *source* applying influence; conformity names the *act* of matching the group with no request necessary. The outline lists them as separate bullets.*
- **Nonnormative behavior** — behavior outside the accepted norms of a group; the outline's own heading for the deviance block.

## 8. 8C (SOC) — Elements of Social Interaction
*Status, role, group, network, organization. Almost every one of these is named on the outline as an explicit pair, which means each is a guaranteed point if you can state the axis.*

### 8.1 Status
- **Status** — any socially defined position a person occupies.
- ★ **Ascribed status** — assigned at birth or involuntarily, without regard to effort (race, sex, age, being someone's daughter).
- ★ **Achieved status** — earned or chosen through action (physician, spouse, convict, college graduate).
  *Split [I18]: did the person do anything to get it? Named as a pair on the outline.*
- ★ **Master status** — the one status that dominates how others perceive a person and organizes all their other statuses.
- **Status set** — the full collection of statuses a person holds at one time.
  *Split [I19]: the single dominant one vs all of them.*
- **Status inconsistency** — holding statuses that rank differently on different dimensions (a highly educated, low-income adjunct).

### 8.2 Role
- **Role** — the set of behaviors and expectations attached to a status. *You **occupy** a status and **perform** a role.*
- **Role set** — all the roles attached to a single status (a physician's roles toward patients, colleagues, insurers).
- ★ **Role conflict** — incompatible demands from **two different roles** (surgeon vs parent, both needed at 6 p.m.).
- ★ **Role strain** — incompatible demands **within a single role** (a physician must be both honest and comforting).
  *Split [I20]: count the roles. Two roles pulling apart = conflict; one role pulling against itself = strain. Named as a pair on the outline — a one-line discrimination worth a guaranteed point.*
- ★ **Role exit** — the process of disengaging from a role central to one's identity and building a new one (leaving the priesthood, retiring, divorcing).
  *Split [I21]: strain is friction *inside* a role you still hold; exit is *leaving* it and rebuilding identity. All three named together on the outline.*
- **Role engulfment** — a role expanding until it absorbs the whole self-concept.

### 8.3 Groups and networks
- **Group** — two or more people who interact and share an identity.
- **Aggregate** — people in the same place at the same time who do not interact (a bus queue).
- **Category** — people who share a trait but neither interact nor gather (all left-handed people).
  *Split [I24]: interaction + identity · co-presence only · shared trait only.*
- ★ **Primary group** — small, intimate, enduring, emotionally close; the relationship is an end in itself (family, close friends).
- ★ **Secondary group** — larger, impersonal, goal-directed, time-limited; the relationship is a means to an end (a project team, a class).
  *Split [I22]: is the relationship the point, or the instrument? Named as a pair on the outline.*
- ★ **In-group** — the group a person identifies with and feels loyalty toward.
- ★ **Out-group** — a group a person does not belong to and may view with competition or hostility.
  *Split [I12]: in-group bias = favoring your own; out-group homogeneity = perceiving *them* as all alike. Two different errors from the same boundary.*
- ★ **Reference group** — the group whose standards you use to evaluate yourself — **membership is not required** (a first-year student comparing themselves to attending physicians).
  *Split [I17]: an in-group is one you belong to; a reference group is one you measure yourself against. This "not necessarily a member" clause is the trap and also the link to relative deprivation [K13].*
- ★ **Dyad** — a two-member group: most intense, most fragile, no majority possible, dies if one leaves.
- ★ **Triad** — a three-member group: coalitions become possible, a third party can mediate, survives the loss of a member.
  *Split [I23]: two vs three changes the *structural possibilities*, not just the headcount. Named on the outline as "group size (e.g., dyads, triads)."*
- **Social network** — the web of ties connecting individuals and groups; the structure through which resources, information, and influence travel.
- **Strong ties / weak ties** — close, frequent, redundant relationships vs distant acquaintances; **weak ties carry novel information** (job leads) because they bridge otherwise separate clusters.
- **Network density** — the proportion of possible ties that actually exist in a network.

### 8.4 Organizations and bureaucracy
- **Formal organization** — any group deliberately structured to pursue stated goals.
- ★ **Bureaucracy** — the specific rational-legal organizational form: hierarchy of authority, explicit written rules, specialized division of labor, impersonality, and merit-based employment.
  *Split [I32]: every bureaucracy is a formal organization; not every formal organization is a bureaucracy. The outline nests bureaucracy under formal organization.*
- **Characteristics of an ideal bureaucracy** — the five above (Weber's ideal type — "ideal" means *pure*, not *good*).
- ★ **Iron law of oligarchy** — every organization, however democratic in intent, drifts toward rule by a small elite.
- ★ **McDonaldization** — society increasingly adopts fast-food principles: **efficiency, calculability, predictability, control** (often through technology replacing human judgment).
  *Split [I33]: who ends up in charge vs what the process comes to look like. Named as the outline's only two "perspectives on bureaucracy," so they are each other's distractor.*
- **Bureaucratic ritualism** — rule-following becomes an end in itself and displaces the organization's goals.
- **Goal displacement** — an organization substitutes new goals (usually its own survival) for its original ones.
- **Utilitarian / normative / coercive organization** — you join for material reward / for a cause you believe in / involuntarily (prison, conscription).

### 8.5 Self-presentation (dramaturgy)
- ★ **Dramaturgical approach** — social life analyzed as theatrical performance: people manage the impression an audience forms of them.
- ★ **Front stage** — behavior performed for an audience, conforming to the role's expectations.
- ★ **Back stage** — behavior when no audience is present and the performance is dropped.
  *Split [I25]: is there an audience whose view of you matters right now? Named verbatim on the outline; AAMC uses it as its own worked example of the SOC depth tag, and one published Preview item is built as a 2×2 on (manipulation type) × (front/back).*
- ★ **Impression management** — the active effort to control the impression others form of you.
- **Self-monitoring** — the *trait* of habitually adjusting one's self-presentation to fit the situation; high self-monitors read the room, low self-monitors act the same everywhere.
  *Split [I26]: an action vs a stable disposition. Impression management is on the outline; self-monitoring is not, and AAMC supplies its definition in the stem when it uses it.*
- **Face / facework** — the public self-image a person claims, and the work done to protect it (yours or someone else's).
- **Verbal communication** — the content of language.
- **Nonverbal communication** — the channel: expression, gesture, posture, proxemics, tone, eye contact.
  *Split [I31]: the outline pairs them, and names **gender and culture** as moderators of both expression *and* detection of emotion.*

## 9. 8A — Self-Identity
*(PSY, SOC) — the smallest content category on the outline by bullet count and one of the most reliably tested. Four terms from one outline bullet do most of the work.*

- **Self-concept** — the total set of beliefs a person holds about who they are.
- ★ **Self-esteem** — the *global evaluation* of one's own worth: how much you like yourself overall.
- ★ **Self-efficacy** — the belief that you can succeed at **a specific task or domain**.
- ★ **Locus of control** — the belief about *where outcomes come from*: your own actions, or luck, fate, and powerful others.
  *Split [G13]: who I think I am · how I rate myself overall · can I do **this** · does anything I do matter. All four sit in a single outline bullet, so all four get offered together.*
  *Split [G14]: self-efficacy is task-specific and about **capability**; self-esteem is global and about **worth**. A person can have high self-esteem and low math self-efficacy. The most commonly conflated pair in 8A.*
- ★ **Internal locus of control** — outcomes follow from my own effort and choices.
- ★ **External locus of control** — outcomes follow from luck, fate, chance, or powerful others.
  *Split [G15]: a stem probing external LOC will use the words "luck," "fate," or "there is nothing I can do." Distinguish from **learned helplessness** [A27], which is an *acquired state* produced by uncontrollable aversive events, not a stable disposition.*
- **Identity** — a self-definition anchored in group membership or social position.
- **Types of identity** — the outline's own examples: race/ethnicity, gender, age, sexual orientation, class. Each is both a demographic category (9B) and an identity (8A).
- ★ **Social identity theory** — self-concept derives partly from group membership, which motivates in-group favoritism and out-group derogation to maintain positive self-regard.
  *Not on AAMC's outline by name, but it is one of the most frequently keyed theory labels in AAMC-published P/S items. **Learn the label.***
- **Personal identity vs social identity** — attributes that are yours alone vs attributes derived from group membership.

### 9.1 Formation of identity
- ★ **Looking-glass self (Cooley)** — self-concept is built from imagining how you appear to others, imagining their judgment, and then feeling about yourself accordingly.
- ★ **Role-taking (Mead)** — learning to see yourself and act by adopting the perspectives of others, developing through **play stage** (one role at a time) → **game stage** (multiple roles at once) → **generalized other** (society's overall perspective).
  *Split [I15]: looking-glass self is about **how you feel about yourself** based on imagined judgment; role-taking is about **learning roles** by occupying others' viewpoints. Named verbatim in the same outline bullet.*
  *Split [I16]: looking-glass self = others' perceptions shape **my self-concept**. Self-fulfilling prophecy = others' expectations shape **my behavior and outcomes**.*
- **"I" and "me" (Mead)** — the spontaneous, acting self vs the internalized attitudes of others toward the self.
- **Generalized other** — the internalized sense of society's overall expectations.
- **Imitation** — reproducing another person's observed behavior; the outline's third named mechanism of individual influence on identity.
- **Reference group** — see [I17]; the standard against which identity is evaluated.
- **Influence of culture and socialization on identity formation** — the outline's own leaf: identity content is supplied by the culture one is socialized into, which is why identity categories vary across societies.

### 9.2 Theories of identity development
*The outline names four types — gender, moral, psychosexual, social — and deliberately removed the theorists' names in 2020. The stages are still testable; the attributions are not asked.*
- ★ **Erikson's psychosocial stages (social)** — eight lifespan crises: trust vs mistrust (infant) · autonomy vs shame/doubt (toddler) · initiative vs guilt (preschool) · industry vs inferiority (school age) · **identity vs role confusion (adolescence)** · **intimacy vs isolation (young adult)** · **generativity vs stagnation (middle adult)** · integrity vs despair (late life).
  *Split [G9/G10]: adjacent stages are the trap. Identity = "who am I." Intimacy = "can I commit to another." Generativity = "am I contributing to the next generation." Integrity = "was my life worthwhile." AAMC has keyed an identity item with generativity descriptions as distractors.*
- ★ **Freud's psychosexual stages** — oral · anal · phallic (Oedipal, superego forms) · latency · genital; fixation at a stage shapes adult personality.
  *Split [G8]: five erogenous-zone conflicts ending in adolescence (Freud) vs eight social crises across the whole lifespan (Erikson).*
- ★ **Kohlberg's moral stages** — **preconventional** (avoid punishment, gain reward — consequences to self) · **conventional** (social approval, then law and order) · **postconventional** (social contract, then abstract universal principles).
  *Split [G11]: read *what justifies the answer*, not what the answer is. "He shouldn't steal because he'd go to jail" is preconventional; "because stealing is illegal" is conventional; "because property rights are less fundamental than life" is postconventional.*
- **Gilligan's care orientation** — a critique of Kohlberg: moral reasoning organized around relationships and care rather than abstract justice. *Not on the outline; useful only as a Kohlberg distractor.*
- **Gender identity development** — the outline's fourth named type: acquisition of gender roles via socialization, modeling, and reinforcement (gender schema).
- **Vygotsky's sociocultural theory** — cognitive and identity development driven by social interaction; **zone of proximal development** (what a child can do with help) and **scaffolding**. Name removed in 2020; survives as "role of culture in cognitive development."

## 10. 8B — Social Thinking
*(PSY, SOC) — attribution, prejudice, and the stereotype processes. Small category, dense in named traps.*

### 10.1 Attribution
- **Attribution** — the process of explaining the cause of a behavior.
- ★ **Dispositional (internal) attribution** — the cause is in the person: their traits, ability, or character.
- ★ **Situational (external) attribution** — the cause is in the circumstances.
- ★ **Fundamental attribution error** — over-attributing **other people's** behavior to disposition while underweighting the situation.
- ★ **Actor–observer bias** — the *asymmetry*: I explain your behavior dispositionally and my own situationally.
  *Split [I5]: FAE is a one-directional error about others; actor–observer names the contrast between how you explain others and yourself.*
- ★ **Self-serving bias** — attributing my successes to myself and my failures to the situation.
  *Split [I6]: FAE is about explaining **others**; self-serving bias is about protecting **my own** self-esteem. Both are standard distractors for each other.*
- **Weiner's attribution dimensions** — **internal/external** × **stable/unstable** × **controllable/uncontrollable**; a 2×2×2 that AAMC can build option sets from directly ("attributed the stressor to a controllable cause").
- **Optimistic vs pessimistic explanatory style** — attributing bad events to external, unstable, specific causes vs internal, stable, global ones; the pessimistic pattern is what learned helplessness generalizes into.
- **Just-world hypothesis** — the belief that people get what they deserve and deserve what they get.
- **Blaming the victim** — the attribution that follows from a just-world belief: the sufferer must have caused their own misfortune.
  *Split [I14]: the underlying belief vs its application to a specific person.*
- **Cultural differences in attribution** — individualist cultures show stronger FAE; collectivist cultures attribute situationally more readily. The outline names "role of culture in attributions" explicitly.
- **Halo effect** — one positive trait (attractiveness) spilling over into unrelated positive judgments.

### 10.2 Prejudice, stereotype, discrimination
- ★ **Stereotype** — a generalized *belief* about the members of a group (cognitive).
- ★ **Prejudice** — a negative *attitude and feeling* toward a group (affective).
- ★ **Discrimination** — unequal *behavior* toward members of a group (behavioral).
  *Split [I1]: think · feel · do. The most-tested triad in 8B/8C, and the outline explicitly names "the relationship between prejudice and discrimination" as its own bullet — meaning items are written on the *link*, including the case where prejudice exists without discrimination and vice versa.*
- ★ **Explicit bias** — conscious, reportable, measured by self-report.
- ★ **Implicit bias** — unconscious, not reportable, measured by reaction time (IAT).
  *Split [I3]: can the person tell you about it? This maps directly onto the measurement item — a Likert scale can only capture explicit bias, which is why studies find effects on the IAT but not the questionnaire.*
- ★ **Modern (subtle, aversive) prejudice** — prejudice that is denied and expressed indirectly, through avoidance or "principled" objections rather than open hostility.
- **Old-fashioned prejudice** — openly stated group hostility.
  *Split [I4]: is it avowed? Modern prejudice is a frequently keyed answer in AAMC-published items.*
- **Ethnocentrism** — judging another culture by your own standards. *(See §3.)*
- **Cultural relativism** — interpreting a culture on its own terms. *(Explicit outline pair.)*
- **Stigma** — a discrediting attribute spoiling social identity; SOC-tagged, so the item wants the social-consequence lens, not the cognitive one.
- **Power, prestige, and class in prejudice** — the outline's SOC-tagged leaf: prejudice is produced and sustained by group position and competition, not only by individual cognition. A conflict-theory framing is often the keyed answer here.
- **Role of emotion in prejudice** — fear, disgust, and threat drive prejudice independently of belief content.
- **Role of cognition in prejudice** — categorization, in-group/out-group sorting, and cognitive shortcuts generate stereotypes automatically.
- **Scapegoating** — displacing frustration onto a weaker out-group.
- **Contact hypothesis** — intergroup contact reduces prejudice under conditions of equal status, common goals, cooperation, and institutional support.

### 10.3 Processes related to stereotypes
- ★ **Self-fulfilling prophecy** — a belief or expectation *causes* the behavior that confirms it; the expectation is held by **someone else about you** (or by you about a situation), and it changes reality.
- ★ **Stereotype threat** — **fear of confirming** a negative stereotype about your own group impairs your performance in that domain.
  *Split [I9]: who holds the belief and what the mechanism is. Self-fulfilling prophecy = an outsider's expectation shapes treatment, which shapes outcome. Stereotype threat = the target's own anxiety about the stereotype consumes working memory. **Named verbatim in the same outline bullet, so they are guaranteed distractors for each other.***
- **Stereotype boost / lift** — a positive stereotype (or an out-group comparison) improving performance.
- **Self-handicapping** — creating an obstacle or excuse in advance so failure can be attributed to it.
  *Split [I10]: anxiety about a group stereotype vs a preemptive excuse for oneself.*
- **Teacher expectancy (Pygmalion effect)** — the institutional form of the self-fulfilling prophecy; cross-reference [J22] in 9A education.

## 11. 7C — Learning and Behavior Change
*AAMC's own published Skill 1 sample item is built here. Nearly every reinforcement/schedule term is named verbatim on the outline.*

### 11.1 Non-associative learning
- ★ **Habituation** — a *decrease* in an innate response after repeated exposure to the same stimulus; no association is learned.
- ★ **Dishabituation** — the recovery of a habituated response when a **new** stimulus is introduced.
- ★ **Sensitization** — an *increase* in responding after an intense or aversive stimulus.
  *Split [A6]: habituation is **non-associative** (an unlearned response declining); extinction is **associative** (a learned association weakening because reinforcement stopped).*
  *Split [A7]: habituation is central/psychological and is reversed by novelty; **sensory adaptation** is at the receptor and is not (your nose stops smelling the room; that is adaptation).*
  *Split [A8]: dishabituation requires a **new stimulus**; **spontaneous recovery** requires only the **passage of time**. Both look like "the response came back" — read the stem for which one the researcher did.*

### 11.2 Classical conditioning
- **Classical (Pavlovian) conditioning** — learning that two stimuli go together, so a previously neutral stimulus comes to elicit an involuntary response.
- **Unconditioned stimulus (US)** — naturally elicits a response without learning (food).
- **Unconditioned response (UR)** — the innate reaction to the US (salivating at food).
- **Neutral stimulus (NS)** — elicits no relevant response before pairing (a bell).
- **Conditioned stimulus (CS)** — the same stimulus *after* pairing, now eliciting a response.
- **Conditioned response (CR)** — the learned reaction to the CS.
  *Split [A13]: NS and CS are the **same object at different phases** — before pairing vs after.*
  *Split [A14]: stimulus = what is done to the organism; response = what the organism does.*
  *Split [A15]: CR and UR are often **physically identical** (both are salivation) — the difference is only which stimulus produced it. That identity is the trap.*
- **Acquisition** — the phase during which the CS–US pairing establishes the CR.
- ★ **Extinction** — the CR weakens and disappears because the CS is presented **without** the US (or, in operant terms, the reinforcer is withheld).
- ★ **Spontaneous recovery** — the extinguished CR reappears after a rest interval, without new pairings.
- **Reacquisition** — the CR returns because the pairing was re-presented; faster than the original acquisition.
  *Split [A9]: did it come back on its own (spontaneous recovery) or was the pairing re-run (reacquisition)?*
- ★ **Stimulus generalization** — responding to stimuli **similar** to the trained CS.
- ★ **Stimulus discrimination** — responding **only** to the trained CS and not to similar ones.
  *Split [A10]: does the response spread outward or stay narrow?*
  *Split [A11]: generalization produces a **learned CR to a similar stimulus**; dishabituation **restores a declining unlearned response** to a novel one. AAMC used exactly this contrast as a distractor in its published Skill 1 item.*
- **Higher-order (second-order) conditioning** — an established CS is used to condition a new neutral stimulus.
- **Blocking** — a new CS fails to become conditioned because an existing CS already predicts the US.
- **Taste aversion (Garcia effect)** — one-trial conditioning of nausea to a flavor across a long delay; the standard evidence for biological preparedness.

### 11.3 Operant conditioning
- **Operant conditioning** — learning in which the *consequences* of a voluntary behavior change how often it occurs.
  *Split [A12]: classical = **involuntary** response, stimulus paired **before** the behavior. Operant = **voluntary** behavior shaped by a consequence that comes **after**. AAMC names this contrast as an explicit Skill 1 target.*
- ★ **Positive reinforcement** — **add** something desirable → behavior **increases**.
- ★ **Negative reinforcement** — **remove** something aversive → behavior **increases**.
- ★ **Positive punishment** — **add** something aversive → behavior **decreases**.
- ★ **Negative punishment (omission)** — **remove** something desirable → behavior **decreases**.
  *Split [A1–A4] — **the single most-missed 2×2 on the section.** Two independent axes: (add / remove) × (increases / decreases). "Positive/negative" means **added/removed, never good/bad.** Reinforcement **always** increases behavior; punishment **always** decreases it. Both negative reinforcement and positive punishment involve an aversive stimulus, which is why they feel the same and are each other's favorite distractor. Drill all four cells, not the diagonal.*
- ★ **Primary reinforcer** — innately reinforcing because it satisfies a biological need (food, water, warmth).
- ★ **Secondary (conditioned) reinforcer** — reinforcing only because it was paired with a primary one (money, grades, praise). *AAMC's outline word is "conditional."*
  *Split [A16]: does it work without learning?*
- ★ **Punishment vs extinction** — punishment **delivers a consequence**; extinction **withholds the reinforcer** and delivers nothing.
  *Split [A5]: presence vs absence of an event. If the stem says "the parent stopped responding," that is extinction, not punishment.*
- **Extinction burst** — a temporary surge in the behavior right after reinforcement is withdrawn.
- ★ **Shaping** — reinforcing successive approximations toward **one** target behavior.
- **Chaining** — linking a **sequence** of separate behaviors, each cueing the next.
  *Split [A22]: one behavior refined vs several behaviors linked.*
- ★ **Escape learning** — behavior that **terminates** an aversive stimulus already present.
- ★ **Avoidance learning** — behavior that **prevents** an anticipated aversive stimulus from arriving.
  *Split [A21]: is the aversive stimulus already here or still coming? Both are negative reinforcement, which is the trap.*
- **Instinctive drift** — a conditioned behavior degrades back toward the species' innate pattern; named on the outline.
- **Biological preparedness** — species are innately predisposed to learn some associations faster than others.
  *Split [A23]: innate behavior **overriding** training vs innate wiring **easing** it.*

### 11.4 Reinforcement schedules
- **Continuous reinforcement** — every response reinforced; fastest acquisition, fastest extinction.
- **Partial (intermittent) reinforcement** — only some responses reinforced; slower acquisition, far more resistant to extinction.
- ★ **Fixed-ratio (FR)** — reinforced after a **set number** of responses; high rate, brief post-reinforcement pause (piecework pay).
- ★ **Variable-ratio (VR)** — reinforced after an **unpredictable number** of responses; **highest, steadiest rate and most extinction-resistant** (slot machines).
- ★ **Fixed-interval (FI)** — reinforced for the first response after a **set time**; produces a **scalloped** pattern of accelerating responding near the deadline (a weekly paycheck, studying before a scheduled exam).
- ★ **Variable-interval (VI)** — reinforced for the first response after an **unpredictable time**; slow, steady, consistent responding (checking email).
  *Split [A17/A18/A19]: first ask **ratio or interval** — does reinforcement depend on how *many* responses or how much *time*? Then ask **fixed or variable** — constant or unpredictable? All four named verbatim on the outline; a true 2×2, so all four cells get offered.*
  *Split [A20]: VR vs FI is the extreme contrast AAMC favors — highest steady rate and hardest to extinguish vs scalloped and easiest.*

### 11.5 Observational and cognitive learning
- ★ **Observational learning (modeling)** — acquiring a behavior by watching someone else perform it, without doing it or being reinforced.
- **Modeling** — the model's demonstration of the behavior.
- **Imitation** — the observer's reproduction of it.
  *Split [A26]: direction of the relationship — who is showing and who is copying.*
- ★ **Vicarious reinforcement / punishment** — seeing the *model* rewarded or punished changes how likely the observer is to perform the behavior.
  *Split [A25]: observational learning is the **acquisition**; vicarious reinforcement is the **motivation to perform** what was acquired.*
- **Bandura's four processes** — attention, retention, reproduction, motivation.
- ★ **Latent learning** — learning that occurs without reinforcement and stays hidden until there is a reason to show it (Tolman's maze rats).
  *Split [A24]: acquired without reinforcement and revealed later vs acquired by watching a model.*
- **Cognitive map** — an internal spatial representation, the evidence for latent learning.
- **Insight learning** — sudden restructuring of a problem into a solution without gradual shaping.
- **Mirror neurons** — neurons firing both when performing an action and when observing another perform it; the outline's named biological substrate for observational learning and vicarious emotion.
- ★ **Learned helplessness** — after exposure to **uncontrollable** aversive events, an organism stops trying even when escape becomes possible.
  *Split [A27]: learned helplessness is an **acquired state** produced by uncontrollability; external locus of control is a **stable disposition**. AAMC names learned helplessness verbatim in its Skill 1 item-type list.*
  *Split [A28]: helplessness is about **contingency** ("nothing I do matters"); low self-efficacy is about **capability** ("I can't do this task").*

### 11.6 Attitude and behavior change
- **Attitude** — an evaluative stance toward a person, object, or idea, with **cognitive, affective, and behavioral** components (the outline's own three).
- ★ **Elaboration likelihood model (ELM)** — persuasion travels one of two routes depending on motivation and ability to think about the message.
- ★ **Central route** — persuasion by argument quality; requires high elaboration; produces durable, resistant attitude change.
- ★ **Peripheral route** — persuasion by surface cues (attractiveness, credibility, number of arguments, mood); low elaboration; produces weak, temporary change.
  *Split [H9]: is the audience motivated and able to think? Named on the outline — one of only two named attitude-change theories, so it is high-probability.*
- ★ **Social cognitive theory** — behavior is learned and changed through the reciprocal interaction of person, behavior, and environment, with observational learning and self-efficacy as the levers. *(The outline's second named attitude-change theory.)*
- ★ **Reciprocal determinism** — person, behavior, and environment continuously cause each other.
  *Split [G16]: reciprocal determinism is **three-way mutual causation**; the outline's "situational approach" says behavior is determined by the situation alone.*
- ★ **Cognitive dissonance theory** — holding inconsistent cognitions (or acting against an attitude) creates discomfort, which is reduced by changing the attitude to match the behavior.
- **Self-perception theory** — you infer your own attitudes by observing your own behavior, with no discomfort required.
  *Split [H10]: is there **arousal/discomfort** driving the change (dissonance) or a cool inference (self-perception)? Cognitive dissonance is named on the outline; self-perception is its standard distractor.*
- ★ **Foot-in-the-door** — a small request first, then the large one; works through commitment and self-concept consistency.
- ★ **Door-in-the-face** — an unreasonable request first, then the real one; works through reciprocal concession.
- **Lowball** — get agreement, then reveal the added cost.
  *Split [H8]: order and mechanism — small→big (commitment) vs big→small (concession) vs agree→cost added. Foot-in-the-door is named verbatim on the outline.*
  *Split [H11]: dissonance changes the **attitude** to match a dissonant act; foot-in-the-door escalates the **behavior** via commitment.*
- **Role-playing effects** — occupying a role changes the attitudes of the person occupying it; named on the outline as a process by which behavior influences attitudes.
- **Justification of effort** — the more it cost you, the more you value it (a dissonance effect).
- ★ **Attitude → behavior vs behavior → attitude** — the outline lists both directions as separate bullets, so **read which direction the item is asking about.** Attitudes best predict behavior when they are specific, strong, stable, and formed by direct experience.
- **Factors affecting attitude change** — message characteristics (quality, repetition, fear appeals), source characteristics (credibility, attractiveness), and target characteristics (involvement, prior knowledge, need for cognition).

## 12. 7B (PSY) — Social Processes Influencing Behavior
*Two of AAMC's four published P/S sample items (Skill 3 and Skill 4) are drawn from this block.*

- ★ **Social facilitation** — the presence of others **improves** performance on simple or well-learned tasks.
- ★ **Social inhibition (impairment)** — the presence of others **worsens** performance on complex or novel tasks.
  *Split [H4]: same mechanism (arousal strengthens the **dominant response**), opposite sign depending on task difficulty. An R4 trap — read whether the task is easy or hard.*
- ★ **Social loafing** — individual effort **drops** when contributions are pooled and individually unidentifiable.
  *Split [H3]: facilitation is about the **presence** of an audience changing your effort on your own task; loafing is about your contribution being **hidden inside a group product**. AAMC's published Skill 3 sample item is a social-loafing study.*
- ★ **Deindividuation** — loss of self-awareness and restraint in a group, producing behavior a person would not perform alone (anonymity, arousal, diffused responsibility).
  *Split [H5]: loafing is reduced **effort**; deindividuation is reduced **restraint**. Both named on the outline.*
- ★ **Bystander effect** — the more bystanders present, the **less likely** and **slower** any one of them is to help.
- ★ **Diffusion of responsibility** — the mechanism: each person assumes someone else will act.
- ★ **Pluralistic ignorance** — the other mechanism: everyone looks calm, so each concludes it is not an emergency.
  *Split [H7]: the phenomenon vs its two mechanisms. AAMC's published Skill 4 sample item asks which correlation supports the bystander effect — the key is that number of bystanders is **positively** correlated with **time to help**.*
- ★ **Conformity** — changing behavior to match a group standard, with **no request made**.
- ★ **Compliance** — agreeing to a **direct request** from someone with no authority over you.
- ★ **Obedience** — following a **direct order** from an authority figure.
  *Split [H1]: was there a request, and did it come from an authority? All three are on the outline and form a standard three-way R2 set.*
- ★ **Normative social influence** — conforming to be **liked and accepted**; produces public compliance without private belief change.
- ★ **Informational social influence** — conforming because you believe the group is **right**; produces genuine private acceptance, and dominates when the task is ambiguous.
  *Split [H2]: fitting in vs being correct. Ambiguity in the stem points to informational; social cost points to normative.*
- **Asch conformity paradigm** — line-judgment; unambiguous task, so conformity there is normative. Conformity falls sharply with a single dissenting ally.
- **Milgram obedience paradigm** — obedience rises with authority legitimacy and proximity, and falls with victim proximity and dissenting peers; also the canonical research-ethics case.
- ★ **Groupthink** — the group suppresses dissent to preserve harmony, producing a poor decision; symptoms include illusion of invulnerability, self-censorship, and mindguards.
- ★ **Group polarization** — group discussion makes the group's **average position more extreme** in the direction it already leaned.
  *Split [H6]: harmony suppressing disagreement vs an existing lean intensifying. Named in the same outline bullet, so they are guaranteed distractors for each other.*
- **Risky shift** — the special case of polarization toward greater risk.
- **Social control / peer pressure / socialization** — see §7 (sociology block).

## 13. 7A — Individual Influences on Behavior
*The largest content category by outline bullet count (~72 bullets). Splits into four blocks: biological bases (see Part III), personality, psychological disorders, and motivation.*

### 13.1 Theories of personality
*AAMC names exactly six perspectives. Expect them offered as a set.*
- ★ **Psychoanalytic** — behavior is driven by **unconscious conflict** among drives and internalized rules, shaped in early childhood.
- ★ **Humanistic** — people are driven toward **growth and self-actualization**; the self-concept and its congruence with experience are central.
- ★ **Trait** — personality is a set of **stable dimensions** on which people differ, measured and used to predict behavior.
- ★ **Social cognitive** — personality emerges from the **reciprocal interaction** of person (beliefs, expectancies, self-efficacy), behavior, and environment.
- ★ **Biological** — personality is grounded in **genetics, temperament, and neurophysiology**.
- ★ **Behaviorist** — there is no inner personality; there are only **learned response patterns** produced by contingencies.
  *Split [G1]: unconscious conflict · growth and self-concept · stable dimensions · person–behavior–environment loop · genes and physiology · contingencies only. All six named verbatim on the outline — the full R2 set.*
  *Split [A29]: behaviorist vs social cognitive — does **cognition mediate** the contingency, or is the contingency doing all the work?*
- **Situational approach** — behavior is explained by the situation rather than by stable traits; the outline names it as its own bullet, opposite the trait perspective.
- **Big Five (OCEAN)** — Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism; the dominant trait model.
- **Id / Ego / Superego** — instinctual drives on the **pleasure principle** / the executive mediator on the **reality principle** / internalized morality and ideals.
- **Defense mechanisms** — unconscious ego strategies for reducing anxiety:
  - ★ **Repression** — unconscious exclusion of a thought from awareness. **Suppression** — deliberate, conscious avoidance of it. *Split [G3]: is it voluntary?*
  - ★ **Projection** — attributing your own unacceptable impulse to someone else. **Displacement** — redirecting the impulse onto a safer target. *Split [G4]: does the impulse move to another **person's mind** or to another **target**?*
  - ★ **Reaction formation** — expressing the **opposite** of the impulse. **Sublimation** — channeling it into something **socially valued**. *Split [G5]: inverted vs redirected productively.*
  - ★ **Rationalization** — inventing an acceptable reason after the fact. **Intellectualization** — stripping the emotion out by treating it abstractly. *Split [G6]: a false justification vs an emotionless analysis.*
  - ★ **Regression** — reverting to an earlier developmental behavior. **Denial** — refusing to accept the reality at all. *Split [G7]: acting younger vs not acknowledging.*
  - **Identification** — adopting the characteristics of a more powerful other.
- **Unconditional positive regard** — acceptance independent of behavior; the humanistic condition for congruence between real and ideal self.
- **Temperament** — early-appearing, biologically based reactivity and emotional style.
- **Personality** — the full developed pattern of thought, feeling, and behavior.
  *Split [G30]: constitutional starting point vs developed outcome. The outline pairs "genes, temperament, and heredity."*

### 13.2 Psychological disorders
- ★ **Biomedical model** — disorder is biological pathology, treated biologically.
- ★ **Biopsychosocial model** — disorder arises from biology **plus** psychology **plus** social context.
  *Split [G27]: named verbatim as a pair on the outline. Any stem mentioning social determinants alongside biology keys biopsychosocial.*
- **Diathesis–stress model** — a predisposition (diathesis) produces disorder only when combined with environmental stress.
- **DSM-5** — the classification system; the outline requires **categories and their distinguishing features, not criteria sets** (no counts, durations, or exclusion rules).
- **Prevalence vs incidence of disorders** — existing cases at a point vs new cases over a period; the outline's "rates of psychological disorders."
- **Anxiety disorders** — generalized anxiety, panic disorder, specific phobia, social anxiety, agoraphobia; excessive fear/anxiety out of proportion to threat.
- ★ **Obsession** — an intrusive, unwanted **thought**. **Compulsion** — a repetitive **act** performed to reduce the anxiety the obsession causes.
  *Split [G21]: thought vs behavior. The outline lists OCD separately from the anxiety disorders — a classification point AAMC can test directly.*
- ★ **PTSD** — trauma-related symptoms persisting **more than one month**.
- **Acute stress disorder** — the same picture lasting **3 days to 1 month**.
  *Split [G22]: duration only.*
- ★ **Somatic symptom disorder** — real, distressing physical symptoms plus excessive thoughts and anxiety about them.
- ★ **Illness anxiety disorder** — preoccupation with **having** a serious illness, with minimal or no somatic symptoms.
- ★ **Conversion disorder (functional neurological)** — neurological deficit (blindness, paralysis) with no organic cause and **no intent**.
- ★ **Factitious disorder** — **intentionally** producing or feigning symptoms to occupy the **sick role**, with no external reward.
- ★ **Malingering** — **intentionally** feigning symptoms for an **external gain** (money, drugs, avoiding work).
  *Split [G24]: unintentional · intentional for the sick role · intentional for external gain. A standard three-way set — the axis is intent, then payoff.*
  *Split [G23]: somatic symptom (symptoms are real and distressing) vs illness anxiety (the fear of illness is the symptom).*
- ★ **Bipolar I** — at least one full **manic** episode, with or without depression.
- ★ **Bipolar II** — **hypomania** plus at least one major depressive episode; never full mania.
  *Split [G19]: does the mood elevation reach full mania (impairment, psychosis, hospitalization)?*
- ★ **Major depressive disorder** — discrete, severe episodes.
- ★ **Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia)** — chronic, lower-grade depressed mood over years.
  *Split [G20]: episodic and severe vs chronic and milder.*
- ★ **Positive symptoms of schizophrenia** — experiences **added**: hallucinations, delusions, disorganized speech and behavior.
- ★ **Negative symptoms** — normal function **subtracted**: flat affect, avolition, alogia, anhedonia, social withdrawal.
  *Split [G17]: added vs removed. "Positive" again means present, not good.*
- ★ **Delusion** — a false **belief**. **Hallucination** — a false **percept**.
  *Split [G18]: thinking vs sensing.*
- **Dissociative identity disorder** — two or more distinct identity states.
- **Dissociative amnesia** — inability to recall autobiographical information, often with fugue.
- **Depersonalization/derealization** — detachment from one's self or from one's surroundings.
  *Split [G25]: identities · memory · sense of reality.*
- ★ **Personality disorder clusters** — **Cluster A** odd/eccentric (paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal) · **Cluster B** dramatic/erratic (antisocial, borderline, histrionic, narcissistic) · **Cluster C** anxious/fearful (avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive PD).
  *Split [G26]: odd · dramatic · anxious. Learn the cluster theme; the outline does not require the criteria.*

### 13.3 Motivation
- **Motivation** — the process that initiates, directs, and sustains goal-directed behavior; the outline's four named factors are **instinct, arousal, drives, needs**.
- ★ **Instinct** — an innate, fixed, species-typical behavior pattern.
- **Drive** — an aroused internal state produced by an unmet biological need, pushing toward homeostasis.
  *Split [F8]: innate fixed pattern vs a homeostatic push state.*
- ★ **Drive-reduction theory** — behavior is **pushed** by internal need states seeking to restore homeostasis.
- ★ **Incentive theory** — behavior is **pulled** by external rewards, independent of internal need.
  *Split [F7]: push from inside vs pull from outside. **Both named on the outline** — the definitive motivation pair.*
- ★ **Arousal theory / Yerkes–Dodson law** — performance is best at an **optimal, intermediate** level of arousal (inverted U), and the optimum is **lower for difficult tasks**.
  *Split [F11]: drive reduction says arousal is always to be minimized; arousal theory says there is a best nonzero level.*
- ★ **Intrinsic motivation** — the activity itself is the reward.
- ★ **Extrinsic motivation** — the reward is external to the activity.
- ★ **Overjustification effect** — an external reward **undermines** pre-existing intrinsic interest, so the behavior drops when the reward stops.
  *Split [F10]: merely external vs external and **corrosive to an interest that was already there**.*
- ★ **Maslow's hierarchy of needs** — physiological → safety → love/belonging → esteem → self-actualization; lower needs generally take priority.
- ★ **Deficiency needs vs growth needs** — the bottom four (motivated by lack) vs self-actualization (motivated by growth).
  *Split [F12/F13]: **esteem** = respect, achievement, recognition, competence in others' and one's own eyes. **Self-actualization** = fulfilling one's unique potential. AAMC's own Skill 2 item-type list turns on **esteem**, not self-actualization — the adjacent-level trap.*
- **Self-determination theory** — three innate needs: autonomy, competence, relatedness.
- **Biological and sociocultural motivators** — the outline names hunger, sex drive, and substance addiction as its three examples; each has a homeostatic/biological component and a learned/cultural one.
- **Negative-feedback system** — the homeostatic mechanism underlying drives: deviation from a set point triggers correction (the outline names it explicitly under drives).
- **Reward pathway** — mesolimbic dopamine (VTA → nucleus accumbens); the named substrate for addiction.

## 14. 6A — Sensing the Environment
*Sensation and perception across all senses. Contains the section's only recurring calculation (Weber's law).*

### 14.1 Psychophysics
- ★ **Sensation** — detection and **transduction** of physical energy into neural signals.
- ★ **Perception** — the **organization and interpretation** of those signals into a meaningful experience.
  *Split [D1]: raw detection vs meaning-making.*
- **Transduction** — conversion of a physical stimulus into an action potential by a receptor.
- ★ **Absolute threshold** — the minimum stimulus intensity detectable **50% of the time**.
- ★ **Difference threshold (JND)** — the minimum **change** in intensity detectable 50% of the time.
- **Subliminal stimulus** — below the absolute threshold; can be processed without awareness but produces only weak priming effects.
  *Split [D2]: detecting anything at all vs detecting a difference.*
- ★ **Weber's law** — the JND is a **constant proportion** of the original stimulus (ΔI/I = k), not a constant amount. *The section's only recurring computation: a 100 g weight with k = 0.02 needs +2 g; a 500 g weight needs +10 g.*
- ★ **Signal detection theory** — detection depends on both **sensitivity (d′)** and a **response criterion**, because signals arrive embedded in noise.
  *Split [D3]: a fixed proportional rule vs detection under noise with a shiftable decision rule.*
- ★ **Sensitivity (d′) vs response bias (criterion)** — how well you can tell signal from noise vs how willing you are to say "yes."
  *Split [D4]: a conservative criterion lowers false alarms **and** hits; it does not change d′. Payoffs and expectations move the criterion, not sensitivity.*
- ★ **Hit / Miss / False alarm / Correct rejection** — signal present + said yes / present + said no / absent + said yes / absent + said no.
  *Split [D5]: a genuine 2×2 (signal present?) × (response yes?) — AAMC's R5 construction applies directly, so all four cells get offered.*
- ★ **Sensory adaptation** — a **receptor-level** decline in responding to an unchanging stimulus.
  *Split [D6]: adaptation is physiological and automatic; selective attention is cognitive filtering; habituation [A7] is a central decline that novelty reverses.*
- **Psychophysics** — the study of the relationship between physical stimulus magnitude and subjective experience.
- **Types of sensory receptors** — **photoreceptors** (light), **mechanoreceptors** (pressure, vibration, stretch, sound, balance), **chemoreceptors** (taste, smell, blood chemistry), **thermoreceptors** (temperature), **nociceptors** (tissue damage / pain), **osmoreceptors** (solute concentration).
- **Sensory pathway** — receptor → afferent (sensory) neuron → spinal cord/brainstem → **thalamus** (all senses except olfaction) → the sense-specific primary cortex → association cortex.
- **Sensory (afferent) vs motor (efferent) neuron** — carries signals **toward** the CNS vs **away from** it.

### 14.2 Vision
- **Cornea → pupil (iris) → lens → retina** — the light path; the lens **accommodates** to focus.
- ★ **Rods** — high sensitivity, low light, peripheral, no color, low acuity; ~120 million.
- ★ **Cones** — low sensitivity, bright light, concentrated in the **fovea**, color and high acuity; ~6 million.
  *Split [D7]: light level, location, and color. Night vision and peripheral detection are rods; reading and color are cones.*
- **Fovea** — the retinal region of maximum acuity, cones only.
- **Optic disc (blind spot)** — where the optic nerve exits; no photoreceptors.
- **Bipolar and ganglion cells** — retinal relay; ganglion axons form the optic nerve.
- **Optic chiasm** — where nasal retinal fibers cross, so each hemisphere receives the **contralateral visual field**.
- **Visual pathway** — retina → optic nerve → chiasm → **lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus** → primary visual cortex (occipital).
- ★ **Parallel processing** — the visual system analyzes **color, form, motion, and depth simultaneously** in separate streams.
- ★ **Feature detection** — cortical neurons tuned to **specific stimulus features** (edges, angles, orientation, movement).
  *Split [D8]: simultaneity across attributes vs specificity of a single neuron's trigger. Both named on the outline.*
- **Dorsal ("where/how") vs ventral ("what") stream** — spatial location and action vs object identity.
- ★ **Trichromatic theory** — three cone types (S/M/L) at the **retina** encode color.
- ★ **Opponent-process theory** — color is coded in **opposing pairs** (red–green, blue–yellow, black–white) **after** the retina; explains afterimages.
  *Split [D9]: these are not rival theories but **successive stages of the same pathway** — that framing is often the keyed answer.*

### 14.3 Hearing and the other senses
- **Outer → middle → inner ear** — pinna and canal → tympanic membrane and ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) → **cochlea**.
- **Hair cells** — the auditory receptors on the **basilar membrane** inside the organ of Corti; bending opens ion channels (transduction).
- **Auditory pathway** — cochlea → auditory (vestibulocochlear, CN VIII) nerve → brainstem → **medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus** → auditory cortex (temporal).
- ★ **Place theory** — **pitch** is coded by **which location** on the basilar membrane vibrates most; works for high frequencies.
- ★ **Frequency (temporal) theory** — pitch is coded by the **rate** at which the whole nerve fires; works for low frequencies. **Volley principle** extends it above single-neuron firing limits.
  *Split [D10]: where vs how fast. High pitch = place; low pitch = frequency.*
- **Conductive vs sensorineural hearing loss** — the outer/middle ear fails to conduct vs hair cells or the auditory nerve are damaged.
- **Somatosensation** — touch, pressure, temperature, and pain; **nociceptors** are the pain receptors.
- ★ **Gate control theory** — spinal interneurons act as a gate that non-painful input and descending signals can close, modulating how much pain reaches the brain.
  *Split [D15]: the sensory channel (nociception) vs the mechanism that modulates it (gate control). Rubbing a bump closes the gate.*
- **Taste (gustation)** — chemoreceptors in taste buds; five qualities: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami.
- **Smell (olfaction)** — olfactory chemoreceptors project to the olfactory bulb and then **directly to the cortex and limbic system, bypassing the thalamus** — the anatomical reason odors cue emotion and memory so strongly.
- **Pheromones** — chemical signals between members of a species that alter behavior; BIO-tagged.
- ★ **Kinesthetic sense (proprioception)** — position and movement of body parts, from receptors in muscles and joints.
- ★ **Vestibular sense** — balance and head orientation, from the **semicircular canals and otolith organs**.
  *Split [D13]: where my limbs are vs which way is up. Both named on the outline.*

### 14.4 Perception
- ★ **Bottom-up processing** — perception built from the **sensory data** upward, with no prior expectation.
- ★ **Top-down processing** — perception driven by **expectation, context, and prior knowledge**.
  *Split [D22 / C22]: is the stem describing raw features assembling, or an expectation shaping what is seen? Reading a smudged word from context is top-down.*
- **Perceptual set** — a readiness to perceive in a particular way, produced by expectation; a top-down effect.
- ★ **Gestalt principles** — grouping rules: **proximity** (near things group), **similarity** (alike things group), **continuity** (smooth paths preferred), **closure** (gaps filled), **figure–ground** (object vs background), **common fate** (things moving together group), **pragnanz** (simplest interpretation wins).
  *Split [D12]: identify which single rule the stimulus exploits. An R2 set — the whole family gets offered.*
- ★ **Perceptual constancy** — an object's size, shape, color, and brightness are perceived as stable despite changing retinal input.
- **Perceptual organization** — the grouping of sensory input into forms, depth, and motion.
  *Split [D14]: stability across change vs assembly into structure.*
- ★ **Monocular depth cues** — relative size, interposition (overlap), linear perspective, texture gradient, relative height, aerial perspective, motion parallax; available to **one eye**.
- ★ **Binocular depth cues** — **retinal disparity** (the two eyes' images differ) and **convergence** (eye muscles turning inward); require **two eyes**.
  *Split [D11]: could you still get this cue with one eye closed?*
- **Phi phenomenon / stroboscopic motion** — apparent motion from sequential static stimuli.
- **Visual cliff** — the classic depth-perception paradigm in infants.

## 15. 6B — Making Sense of the Environment
*The largest FC6 category: attention, cognition, consciousness, memory, language. Memory alone is heavily represented in AAMC-published items.*

### 15.1 Attention
- ★ **Selective attention** — focusing on one input channel and filtering the rest.
- ★ **Divided attention** — splitting attention across several tasks at once; possible only when at least one is automatic.
  *Split [C20]: one channel vs several. Both named on the outline.*
- **Cocktail-party effect** — salient unattended information (your own name) breaking through the filter.
- **Inattentional blindness** — failing to see an unexpected object because attention is elsewhere.
- **Change blindness** — failing to notice a change in an unattended part of a scene.
  *Split [C21]: something got through the filter vs something never got in.*
- **Controlled vs automatic processing** — effortful, capacity-limited, serial vs effortless, capacity-free, parallel.

### 15.2 Cognition and cognitive development
- ★ **Information-processing model** — cognition as **continuous** growth in encoding speed, capacity, and strategy, on an input → processing → storage → output architecture.
  *Split [C23]: continuous quantitative growth vs Piaget's **discrete qualitative stages** — both named under Cognition on the outline, so they are each other's distractor.*
- ★ **Schema** — an organized knowledge framework that guides how new information is interpreted and stored.
- ★ **Assimilation** — fitting new experience **into** an existing schema without changing it.
- ★ **Accommodation** — **modifying** the schema because the new experience does not fit.
  *Split [C11]: does the schema change? **The classic P/S pair**, named on the outline under Piaget. Calling a horse "doggy" is assimilation; learning that horses are a separate category is accommodation.*
  *Split [C12]: Piagetian assimilation vs **sociological** assimilation [§3] is a true cross-domain homonym — both are on the outline, and an option set can mix them.*
- ★ **Sensorimotor (0–2)** — knowing through action and sensation; achievement is **object permanence**.
- ★ **Preoperational (2–7)** — symbolic thought and language, but **egocentric** and **lacking conservation**; animism, centration, irreversibility.
- ★ **Concrete operational (7–11)** — logical operations on **concrete** objects; **conservation** achieved, reversibility, classification.
- ★ **Formal operational (11+)** — **abstract and hypothetical** reasoning, systematic hypothesis testing.
  *Split [C13/C15/C16]: object permanence is sensorimotor; conservation failure is preoperational; conservation success with concrete material is concrete operational; abstract "what if" reasoning is formal operational. **AAMC's Skill 1 item-type list names the conservation-problem → stage item verbatim** — expect the adjacent-stage distractor.*
- ★ **Egocentrism** — inability to take another's perspective (preoperational marker).
- **Theory of mind** — the ability to attribute mental states (beliefs, desires) to others, typically ~4 years.
  *Split [C14]: cannot take the perspective vs can attribute mental states.*
- **Conservation** — quantity is unchanged by a change in appearance.
- **Centration** — focusing on one salient dimension and ignoring others.
- **Cognitive changes in late adulthood** — processing speed and working memory decline; vocabulary and accumulated knowledge hold or improve; recognition holds better than free recall.
- **Role of culture in cognitive development** — cognition develops through socially mediated activity; scaffolding within the **zone of proximal development** (what a learner can do with help but not alone).
- **Heredity and environment in cognitive development** — the outline requires the interaction framing, not a nature-or-nurture answer.

### 15.3 Problem solving, heuristics, intelligence
- ★ **Algorithm** — a step-by-step procedure that **guarantees** a solution but is slow.
- ★ **Heuristic** — a mental shortcut that is **fast but fallible**.
  *Split [C7]: guaranteed vs fast. Named under "approaches to problem-solving."*
- ★ **Availability heuristic** — judging likelihood by **how easily examples come to mind** (vivid, recent, or emotional events feel more common).
- ★ **Representativeness heuristic** — judging likelihood by **how well something matches a prototype**, ignoring base rates.
  *Split [C1]: ease of recall vs resemblance. **The single most-tested cognition pair**; the outline names "heuristics and biases."*
- ★ **Base-rate fallacy** — ignoring the underlying frequency of a category in favor of individuating information.
  *Split [C2]: representativeness names the **shortcut used**; base-rate fallacy names the **information ignored**. Two descriptions of one error.*
- ★ **Confirmation bias** — seeking and weighting evidence that supports what you already believe.
- ★ **Belief perseverance** — continuing to hold a belief **after** the evidence for it has been discredited.
  *Split [C3]: gathering evidence selectively vs refusing to update after refutation. **Belief perseverance is named verbatim on the outline.***
- ★ **Overconfidence** — overestimating the **accuracy of your own judgments**.
- **Optimism bias** — underestimating your **personal risk** of bad outcomes.
  *Split [C4]: my judgments are right vs bad things happen to other people. **Overconfidence is named verbatim on the outline.***
- **Hindsight bias** — "I knew it all along" after the outcome is known.
  *Split [C5]: retroactive certainty vs continued belief after disconfirmation.*
- ★ **Framing effect** — the same information presented as a gain or a loss changes the decision.
- ★ **Anchoring** — an initial number drags subsequent estimates toward it.
  *Split [C9]: how the option is worded vs a starting number contaminating the estimate.*
- ★ **Functional fixedness** — fixating on an object's **customary use** and failing to see another.
- ★ **Mental set** — persisting with a **previously successful approach** when a better one exists.
  *Split [C6]: fixated on an **object** vs fixated on a **method**. Both are outline "barriers to effective problem-solving."*
- **Convergent thinking** — narrowing to the one correct answer.
- **Divergent thinking** — generating many possibilities; the creativity measure.
- **Trial and error / means–ends analysis / working backward** — the outline's "types of problem-solving."
- **Intuition** — fast, automatic, non-conscious judgment.
- ★ **Fluid intelligence** — reasoning about novel problems; **declines** with age.
- ★ **Crystallized intelligence** — accumulated knowledge and vocabulary; **holds or rises** with age.
  *Split [C10]: novel vs stored. The outline's "cognitive changes in late adulthood" is essentially this pair.*
- **General intelligence (g)** — a single underlying factor accounting for correlated performance across tasks (Spearman).
- **Multiple intelligences** — several independent domains (linguistic, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic) (Gardner).
- **Triarchic theory** — analytical, creative, practical intelligence (Sternberg).
- **Heredity and environment in intelligence** — heritability estimates are population-specific and do not license claims about between-group differences; the outline names environmental influence explicitly.
- **Variations in intellectual ability** — the outline's leaf: the normal distribution of IQ, intellectual disability, and giftedness; **IQ = (mental age / chronological age) × 100** historically, deviation IQ now (mean 100, SD 15).
- **Stereotype threat in testing** — cross-reference [I9]; a named mechanism for measured group gaps.

### 15.4 Consciousness, sleep, and drugs
- **Consciousness** — awareness of oneself and one's environment.
- **Alertness** — the waking state maintained by the **reticular activating system**.
- ★ **Beta waves** — alert, awake, engaged (high frequency, low amplitude).
- ★ **Alpha waves** — awake but relaxed, eyes closed.
- ★ **Theta waves** — NREM stage 1, light sleep; hypnagogic jerks.
- ★ **Sleep spindles and K-complexes** — NREM stage 2.
- ★ **Delta waves** — NREM stage 3 (slow-wave sleep), deepest; growth hormone release; night terrors and sleepwalking occur here.
  *Split [E2]: frequency falls and amplitude rises as sleep deepens — beta → alpha → theta → delta. An R2 set.*
- ★ **REM sleep** — fast, desynchronized, **beta-like** EEG plus muscle **atonia** plus vivid dreaming; "paradoxical sleep."
  *Split [E1]: REM looks awake on EEG but the body is paralyzed; SWS looks maximally asleep on EEG but the body can move (hence parasomnias).*
- ★ **Circadian rhythm** — the ~24-hour cycle governed by the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** and melatonin from the pineal gland.
- ★ **Sleep cycle** — the ~90-minute cycle *within* sleep; REM periods lengthen across the night, SWS shortens.
  *Split [E5]: a day-length rhythm vs a within-night cycle. Both named on the outline.*
- ★ **Insomnia** — persistent difficulty falling or staying asleep.
- ★ **Narcolepsy** — sudden uncontrollable sleep attacks with **REM intrusion**; cataplexy; hypocretin/orexin loss.
- ★ **Sleep apnea** — repeated cessation of breathing during sleep with arousals and daytime sleepiness.
  *Split [E3]: cannot sleep · sleeps suddenly · stops breathing.*
- ★ **Night terrors** — NREM (SWS), early night, no dream recall, hard to wake.
- ★ **Nightmares** — REM, late night, vivid recall, easy to wake.
  *Split [E4]: which sleep stage, and is it remembered?*
- **Dreaming** — activation-synthesis (the cortex interprets random pontine activity) vs consolidation vs wish-fulfillment accounts.
- ★ **Hypnosis** — a state of heightened **suggestibility** under focused attention, induced by another.
- ★ **Meditation** — self-directed attentional training producing relaxation and altered awareness.
  *Split [E9]: induced by someone else vs self-directed. Named together on the outline.*
- ★ **Depressants** — alcohol, barbiturates, benzodiazepines; enhance **GABA**, reduce CNS activity.
- ★ **Stimulants** — cocaine, amphetamines, caffeine, nicotine; increase **dopamine/norepinephrine**, raise CNS activity.
- ★ **Opiates/opioids** — heroin, morphine; bind endorphin receptors → analgesia and euphoria; depress respiration.
- ★ **Hallucinogens** — LSD, psilocybin; serotonergic; perceptual distortion without sedation or stimulation.
  *Split [E7]: CNS down · CNS up · pain and euphoria · perception distorted.*
- ★ **Tolerance** — needing more of the drug for the same effect.
- ★ **Withdrawal** — aversive symptoms on cessation, typically opposite to the drug's effect.
- ★ **Dependence** — the adapted physiological state that produces tolerance and withdrawal.
- ★ **Addiction** — compulsive use despite harm; a behavioral pattern, not a physiological measurement.
  *Split [E6]: dose escalation · symptoms on stopping · physiological adaptation · compulsive behavior. An R2 set.*
- ★ **Physical dependence** — withdrawal symptoms. **Psychological dependence** — craving and emotional reliance.
  *Split [E8]: body vs want.*
- **Reward pathway** — mesolimbic dopamine, VTA → nucleus accumbens; all addictive drugs converge on it. The outline names it explicitly.

### 15.5 Memory
- **Encoding → storage → retrieval** — the three memory operations the outline is organized around.
- **Sensory memory** — a very brief, high-capacity buffer.
- ★ **Iconic memory** — visual sensory store, ~0.25–0.5 s.
- ★ **Echoic memory** — auditory sensory store, ~3–4 s.
  *Split [B9]: which modality, and how long.*
- ★ **Short-term memory** — passive holding, ~7±2 items, ~15–30 s without rehearsal.
- ★ **Working memory** — active **manipulation**: central executive plus phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, episodic buffer.
  *Split [B8]: holding vs doing something with it.*
- **Long-term memory** — effectively unlimited capacity and duration.
- ★ **Explicit (declarative) memory** — consciously retrievable and statable; hippocampus-dependent.
- ★ **Implicit (nondeclarative) memory** — expressed through performance without conscious recall; includes procedural skills, priming, conditioned responses; cerebellum and basal ganglia.
  *Split [B5]: can you say it?*
- ★ **Episodic memory** — a personally experienced **event**, with time and place.
- ★ **Semantic memory** — a **context-free fact**.
  *Split [B6]: is it tied to your own experience? AAMC has keyed episodic with semantic as the distractor, and offered the whole taxonomy (sensory/semantic/procedural/episodic) as an R2 set.*
- ★ **Procedural memory** — how to do something; implicit.
  *Split [B7]: skill vs fact.*
- ★ **Maintenance rehearsal** — repeating information to keep it active; shallow.
- ★ **Elaborative rehearsal** — connecting new information to existing knowledge; deep, and far better for long-term retention.
  *Split [B16]: repetition vs connection.*
- ★ **Levels of processing: shallow vs deep** — structural/phonemic encoding vs **semantic** encoding; deeper = better recall.
  *Split [B17]: what the encoding task made you attend to.*
- **Chunking** — regrouping items into meaningful units to beat the STM capacity limit.
- **Method of loci** — encoding items onto a familiar spatial route.
- **Mnemonic** — any deliberate memory aid.
  *Split [B18]: regrouping · spatial imagery · the general category.*
- **Self-reference effect** — material related to oneself is remembered better.
- **Dual coding** — encoding both verbally and visually improves retention.
- **Spacing effect** — distributed practice beats massed practice.
- ★ **Recall** — producing information with no cue present.
- ★ **Recognition** — identifying previously encountered information from options.
- ★ **Relearning (savings)** — re-acquiring known material faster the second time.
  *Split [B4]: produce it · pick it · re-learn it faster. All three named together on the outline.*
- **Retrieval cue** — any stimulus that aids access to a stored memory.
- **Priming** — prior exposure to a stimulus speeding later processing of a related one.
- **Spreading activation** — activating one node in a **semantic network** partially activates connected nodes; the mechanism behind priming.
  *Split [B13]: the observed effect vs the network mechanism producing it. Both concepts sit under the outline's "semantic networks and spreading activation."*
- **Semantic network** — concepts stored as nodes linked by associations.
- **Schema** — an organized framework that guides interpretation and can **distort** what is recalled.
  *Split [B21]: an association structure vs an interpretive framework.*
- ★ **Context-dependent memory** — retrieval improves when the **external environment** matches encoding.
- ★ **State-dependent memory** — retrieval improves when the **internal state** (mood, intoxication) matches encoding.
  *Split [B15]: outside vs inside. Mood-congruent memory is the affective special case.*
- ★ **Primacy effect** — better recall of the **first** items (rehearsed into long-term store).
- ★ **Recency effect** — better recall of the **last** items (still in short-term store).
  *Split [B14]: a delay before recall **eliminates recency but not primacy** — that manipulation is the standard item.*
- ★ **Proactive interference** — **old** learning blocks **new**.
- ★ **Retroactive interference** — **new** learning blocks **old**.
  *Split [B1]: "pro" points forward in time (old interferes forward onto new); "retro" points backward. **AAMC names retroactive interference verbatim in its Skill 1 item-type list.***
- ★ **Decay** — the memory trace fades with the mere **passage of time**, no competing material involved.
  *Split [B2]: was a competing item introduced (interference) or just a delay (decay)? AAMC has keyed decay with interference as the distractor.*
- ★ **Encoding failure vs storage decay vs retrieval failure** — it never got in / it faded / it is there but inaccessible right now.
  *Split [B3]: tip-of-the-tongue is retrieval failure — the strongest evidence that storage is intact.*
- ★ **Memory construction / reconstruction** — memory is rebuilt at retrieval, not replayed, so it can incorporate new material.
- ★ **Misinformation effect** — post-event information alters the **content** of the memory.
- ★ **Source-monitoring error** — the content is right but its **origin** is misattributed.
  *Split [B11]: was the memory's content changed, or only its source? The outline names "memory construction and source monitoring."*
- **Flashbulb memory** — a vividly detailed, highly confident memory of a surprising emotional event — **and no more accurate than ordinary memory**, which is the tested point. Amygdala-dependent.
- **Eidetic memory** — genuinely photographic detail.
  *Split [B12]: vivid and confident but not accurate vs literally detailed.*
- ★ **Anterograde amnesia** — cannot form **new** memories (hippocampal damage); implicit learning often survives.
- ★ **Retrograde amnesia** — cannot retrieve memories from **before** the event.
  *Split [B10]: forward vs backward from the injury.*
- ★ **Alzheimer's disease** — progressive; **beta-amyloid plaques and tau tangles**, hippocampal and cortical atrophy, **acetylcholine** loss; anterograde memory loss first.
- ★ **Korsakoff's syndrome** — **thiamine (B1) deficiency**, usually alcohol-related; anterograde amnesia with **confabulation**.
  *Split [B19]: progressive degenerative disease vs a nutritional deficiency; confabulation is the Korsakoff tell. Both named on the outline.*
- **Aging and memory** — episodic and free recall decline; semantic, procedural, and recognition are relatively preserved.
- ★ **Long-term potentiation (LTP)** — persistent strengthening of a synapse after repeated high-frequency stimulation; glutamate/NMDA-mediated. **The named cellular mechanism of learning and memory.**
- **Neural plasticity** — the brain's general capacity to reorganize structure and function with experience.
  *Split [B20]: a specific synaptic mechanism vs the general capacity.*
- **Role of emotion in retrieval** — amygdala activation at encoding enhances consolidation of emotional material (flashbulb memories, stress-hormone modulation).

### 15.6 Language
- **Phoneme → morpheme → semantics → syntax → pragmatics** — smallest sound unit → smallest meaning unit → meaning → grammar rules → social use.
- **Language milestones** — cooing (~2 mo) → babbling (~6 mo) → one-word/holophrastic (~12 mo) → telegraphic two-word (~18–24 mo) → overgeneralization of grammar rules ("goed").
- ★ **Learning (behaviorist) theory of language** — language is acquired through imitation and reinforcement.
- ★ **Nativist theory** — humans have an innate **language acquisition device** and universal grammar; the **poverty of the stimulus** argument; a **critical period** for acquisition.
- ★ **Interactionist theory** — biological readiness plus social interaction together produce language.
  *Split [C17]: **all three named verbatim on the outline** — expect the full three-way set.*
- ★ **Linguistic relativity (Sapir–Whorf)** — the language you speak shapes how you think and perceive.
  *Split [C18]: language **shapes thought** (relativity) vs the **capacity for language is innate** (nativism). The outline's "influence of language on cognition."*
- ★ **Broca's area** — left frontal; **speech production**. Damage → **non-fluent (expressive) aphasia**: effortful, telegraphic speech, comprehension largely intact, and the patient is aware of the deficit.
- ★ **Wernicke's area** — left temporal; **language comprehension**. Damage → **fluent (receptive) aphasia**: effortless but meaningless speech, poor comprehension, and the patient is often unaware.
  *Split [C19 / N10]: production vs comprehension, and fluent vs non-fluent. **Conduction aphasia** = arcuate fasciculus damage, comprehension and fluency intact but repetition impaired.*

## 16. 6C — Responding to the World: Emotion and Stress
*Small category (~21 bullets), and the theories-of-emotion triad is the most reliably recurring "which theory" item on the section.*

### 16.1 Emotion
- ★ **Three components of emotion** — **cognitive** (appraisal, subjective label), **physiological** (autonomic arousal), **behavioral** (expression, action tendency). The outline names exactly these three.
- ★ **Universal emotions** — the outline's own list: fear, anger, happiness, surprise, joy, disgust, sadness; recognized across cultures from facial expression.
- **Adaptive role of emotion** — emotions rapidly prepare action, guide decisions, and signal intent to others.
- ★ **James–Lange theory** — the stimulus triggers **physiological arousal first**, and the emotion **is** the perception of that arousal. *("I am afraid because I am trembling.")*
- ★ **Cannon–Bard theory** — the thalamus sends signals so that arousal and the conscious emotion occur **simultaneously and independently**. *("I tremble and feel afraid at the same time.")*
- ★ **Schachter–Singer two-factor theory** — arousal **plus a cognitive label** drawn from context produces the emotion; the same arousal can become different emotions depending on interpretation.
  *Split [F1]: **arousal → emotion · arousal and emotion together · arousal + label → emotion.** All three named verbatim on the AAMC outline — **the single most reliable "which theory" item on the section.** The tell for Schachter–Singer is that the stem supplies an ambiguous or misattributed context.*
- **Lazarus appraisal theory** — **cognitive appraisal comes first** and determines both the arousal and the emotion.
  *Split [F2]: Schachter–Singer labels arousal **after** it happens; Lazarus appraises the stimulus **before** arousal.*
- **Misattribution of arousal** — arousal from one source being labeled as an emotion caused by another (the shaky-bridge effect); the direct prediction of two-factor theory.
- **Facial feedback hypothesis** — facial expression itself feeds back to intensify or generate the felt emotion.
- **Display rules** — culturally learned norms about which emotions may be shown, to whom, and how; the outline's "role of culture" in expressing emotion.
- **Role of gender in expressing and detecting emotion** — a named outline moderator (8C): socialized differences in expression and in decoding accuracy.
- **Limbic system in emotion** — **amygdala** (fear, threat detection, emotional salience), **hippocampus** (contextual memory for the emotion), **hypothalamus** (autonomic and endocrine output), **cingulate cortex** (emotional regulation and conflict), **prefrontal cortex** (appraisal, regulation, inhibition).
- **Emotion and the autonomic nervous system** — sympathetic activation is the physiological component; the outline names it directly.
- **Physiological markers (signatures) of emotion** — heart rate, skin conductance, cortisol, facial EMG, startle magnitude; the reason P/S passages contain physiological dependent variables.

### 16.2 Stress
- ★ **Stress** — the process by which a person appraises and responds to events perceived as threatening or exceeding their resources.
- ★ **Stressor** — the event; **stress reaction** — the response.
- ★ **Primary appraisal** — "**Is this a threat?**" (irrelevant, benign, or stressful — and if stressful: harm, threat, or challenge).
- ★ **Secondary appraisal** — "**Can I cope with it?**" — an evaluation of one's own resources.
  *Split [F3]: what the event is vs what I can do about it. The outline names appraisal explicitly under "the nature of stress."*
- **Types of stressors** — the outline's examples: **cataclysmic events** (sudden, shared, large-scale), **personal stressors** (major individual life events), plus **daily hassles** and **ambient stressors** (chronic background: noise, crowding, pollution).
- ★ **Eustress** — stress appraised as a challenge; motivating and performance-enhancing.
- ★ **Distress** — stress appraised as a threat; harmful.
  *Split [F4]: appraisal determines which one the same event becomes.*
- ★ **General adaptation syndrome (GAS)** — **alarm** (initial sympathetic/adrenal surge) → **resistance** (sustained elevated cortisol, apparent coping) → **exhaustion** (resources depleted; illness, immunosuppression).
  *Split [F5]: which phase does the physiological description fit? An R2 set.*
- ★ **HPA axis** — hypothalamus → CRH → anterior pituitary → ACTH → adrenal **cortex** → **cortisol**; slow and sustained.
- ★ **Sympathomedullary (SAM) pathway** — sympathetic nervous system → adrenal **medulla** → **epinephrine/norepinephrine**; fast and short-lived.
  *Split [F16 / N15]: cortisol is the slow chronic-stress hormone; epinephrine is the fast acute one. A passage that measures cortisol is measuring sustained stress, not a startle.*
- **Effects of stress on psychological functions** — narrowed attention, impaired working memory and hippocampal function, reduced flexible problem-solving, increased use of dominant/habitual responses.
- **Effects of chronic stress on health** — immunosuppression, cardiovascular disease, metabolic dysregulation, accelerated aging; the mechanism behind the SES–health gradient [L14].
- ★ **Problem-focused coping** — acting on the **stressor** to change it; effective when the stressor is controllable.
- ★ **Emotion-focused coping** — regulating one's **response** to the stressor; effective when it is not controllable.
- **Avoidant coping** — disengaging from the stressor entirely; generally associated with worse outcomes.
  *Split [F6]: change the situation vs change your reaction vs leave the field. AAMC has used "problem-focused rather than avoidant coping" verbatim in a published passage.*
- **Managing stress** — the outline's own examples: exercise, relaxation, spirituality; add social support and cognitive reappraisal.
- **Social support** — emotional, informational, instrumental (tangible), and appraisal support; buffers the health effects of stress.

## 17. 8C (PSY) — Social Behavior
*Attraction, aggression, attachment, altruism, and the animal-behavior residue AAMC keeps in scope.*

- **Attraction** — the outline's leaf; the named determinants are **mere exposure** (familiarity breeds liking), **proximity** (physical nearness), **similarity** (matched attitudes and traits), **reciprocal liking**, and **physical attractiveness**.
  *Split [I28]: identify which single mechanism the vignette activates — an R2 set.*
- **Mere exposure effect** — repeated exposure alone increases liking.
- **Aggression** — behavior intended to harm; **instrumental** (goal-directed, cold) vs **hostile** (emotion-driven, hot). Amygdala, testosterone, low serotonin, and frustration are the named biological and situational contributors.
- **Frustration–aggression hypothesis** — blocked goal pursuit produces aggression, often displaced onto a safer target.
- ★ **Attachment** — the enduring emotional bond between infant and caregiver.
- ★ **Secure attachment** — distressed at separation, comforted at reunion; sensitive, consistent caregiving.
- ★ **Avoidant attachment** — little distress at separation, avoids caregiver at reunion; unresponsive caregiving.
- ★ **Ambivalent (anxious-resistant) attachment** — intense distress, then simultaneously seeks and resists comfort; inconsistent caregiving.
- ★ **Disorganized attachment** — contradictory, dazed, fearful behavior; associated with frightening caregiving.
  *Split [I29]: **read the reunion, not the separation.** The Strange Situation classifies on how the infant behaves when the caregiver returns.*
- **Contact comfort** — infant attachment forms around soft contact rather than feeding (Harlow); the evidence against the drive-reduction account of attachment.
- ★ **Altruism** — helping at a cost to oneself with no expected return.
- ★ **Reciprocal altruism** — helping with the expectation that the favor will be repaid later.
- ★ **Inclusive fitness (kin selection)** — behavior that lowers one's own fitness but raises the fitness of relatives, propagating shared genes.
  *Split [I27]: no return · delayed return · genetic return. Altruism and inclusive fitness are both on the outline; inclusive fitness is BIO-tagged, so an item using it will be framed evolutionarily.*
- **Empathy–altruism hypothesis** — empathy produces genuinely other-oriented helping, distinct from egoistic distress-reduction.
- **Social support** — emotional, informational, instrumental, appraisal; PSY and SOC tagged.
  *Split [I30]: comfort · advice and information · tangible aid · feedback for self-evaluation.*
- **Foraging behavior** — BIO-tagged; optimal foraging as a cost-benefit model of animal behavior.
- **Mating behavior and mate choice** — intersexual selection (choosing) vs intrasexual selection (competing); parental investment theory.
- **Game theory applications** — BIO-tagged; the prisoner's dilemma and evolutionarily stable strategies as models of cooperation and conflict.
- **Animal signals and communication** — honest signals, costly signaling, alarm calls; PSY/BIO-tagged.

# PART III — BIOLOGY INSIDE P/S

*7A biological bases of behavior, plus the BIO-tagged leaves in 6A and 6B. ~5% of the section is pure intro biology **plus** a further ~5% is "biologically relevant psychology" — so roughly 6 questions. AAMC-published items lean heavily on one pair: amygdala vs hippocampus.*

## 18. Nervous system organization
- ★ **Central nervous system (CNS)** — brain and spinal cord.
- ★ **Peripheral nervous system (PNS)** — everything else.
- ★ **Somatic nervous system** — voluntary control of skeletal muscle plus sensory afferents.
- ★ **Autonomic nervous system** — involuntary control of smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands.
  *Split [N2]: CNS/PNS and somatic/autonomic are **two different partitions**, not four parallel categories. AAMC builds distractors by mixing the two levels — that is a level-of-analysis trap, so check which partition the stem is using before comparing options.*
- ★ **Sympathetic** — mobilize: "fight or flight." ↑HR, ↑BP, bronchodilation, pupil dilation, glucose release, ↓digestion.
- ★ **Parasympathetic** — conserve: "rest and digest." ↓HR, ↑digestion, pupil constriction; largely vagal.
  *Split [N1]: mobilize vs conserve. AAMC has keyed sympathetic with **CNS** and **somatic** offered as distractors — the wrong axis entirely.*
- **Reflex arc** — sensory neuron → interneuron in the spinal cord → motor neuron; produces a response without brain involvement.
- **Neuron structure** — dendrites (receive) → soma → axon hillock (integration and threshold) → axon → terminal buttons; myelin from oligodendrocytes (CNS) and Schwann cells (PNS) speeds conduction by **saltatory** transmission at nodes of Ranvier.
- **Action potential** — all-or-none depolarization; resting ≈ −70 mV, threshold ≈ −55 mV, Na⁺ in → depolarize, K⁺ out → repolarize, refractory period.
- **Synaptic transmission** — vesicular release into the cleft → receptor binding → **EPSP or IPSP** → reuptake, enzymatic degradation, or diffusion.
- **Excitatory vs inhibitory postsynaptic potential** — depolarizing (makes firing more likely) vs hyperpolarizing (less likely); summation at the axon hillock decides.

## 19. Brain regions
- ★ **Hindbrain** — **medulla** (breathing, heart rate, vomiting, swallowing), **pons** (sleep, arousal, facial control, relay), **cerebellum** (balance, coordination, procedural motor learning).
- ★ **Midbrain** — **inferior and superior colliculi** (auditory and visual reflex orienting), substantia nigra, reticular formation.
- ★ **Forebrain** — thalamus, hypothalamus, limbic system, basal ganglia, cerebral cortex.
  *Split [N11]: the outline's own three-way division — an R2 set. Rule of thumb: vital automatic functions are hindbrain; sensorimotor reflexes are midbrain; everything cognitive, emotional, and endocrine is forebrain.*
- ★ **Thalamus** — sensory relay station for **every sense except olfaction**.
- ★ **Hypothalamus** — homeostasis and drives: hunger (lateral = hunger on, ventromedial = satiety), thirst, temperature, sex, circadian (SCN), and endocrine control of the pituitary.
  *Split [N4]: relay vs regulation. "The four F's" are hypothalamus.*
- ★ **Amygdala** — fear, threat detection, aggression, and the **emotional salience** tag that enhances memory consolidation.
- ★ **Hippocampus** — formation of **new declarative memories** and spatial memory; damage → anterograde amnesia.
  *Split [N3]: **emotion vs memory formation.** This is the single most-used brain pair in AAMC-published P/S items — it has appeared as a key or a distractor repeatedly, including flashbulb memory → amygdala.*
- ★ **Prefrontal cortex** — executive function, planning, working memory, impulse inhibition, personality; matures into the mid-20s.
  *Split [N5]: forming new memories vs planning and inhibiting. A stem about poor impulse control is PFC, not hippocampus.*
- **Basal ganglia** — voluntary motor control, procedural learning, habit formation; degenerate in Parkinson's and Huntington's.
- **Cerebral lobes** — **frontal** (motor, executive, Broca's), **parietal** (somatosensory, spatial), **temporal** (auditory, Wernicke's, memory), **occipital** (visual).
- **Primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus)** vs **primary somatosensory cortex (postcentral gyrus)** — output vs input, both organized as a homunculus.
- ★ **Lateralization** — **left hemisphere**: language, analytic and sequential processing (in most people). **Right hemisphere**: spatial, holistic, emotional prosody, face processing. Communication is via the **corpus callosum**; split-brain findings follow from cutting it.
- **Reticular activating system** — brainstem network maintaining arousal, alertness, and sleep–wake transitions.
- ★ **Methods of studying the brain** — **EEG** (electrical, millisecond time resolution, poor spatial), **fMRI** (BOLD signal, good spatial, seconds), **PET** (metabolic tracer), **CT/structural MRI** (structure only), **lesion studies and TMS** (causal but crude).
  *Split [N13]: what the method actually measures and its resolution trade-off. If the stem asks "which method shows *when*," it is EEG; "*where*," fMRI; "does region X *cause* the behavior," a lesion or TMS.*

## 20. Neurotransmitters and endocrine
- ★ **Dopamine** — reward, motivation, movement. **Excess mesolimbic** → schizophrenia positive symptoms; **nigrostriatal loss** → Parkinson's; the addiction pathway.
- ★ **Serotonin** — mood, sleep, appetite, aggression regulation; deficits implicated in depression and OCD.
  *Split [N6]: motivation/movement vs mood/sleep/appetite.*
- ★ **Acetylcholine** — neuromuscular junction, parasympathetic postganglionic, attention and memory; **lost in Alzheimer's**.
- ★ **Norepinephrine** — arousal, alertness, sympathetic activation; deficits implicated in depression.
  *Split [N7]: muscle and memory vs arousal and vigilance.*
- ★ **GABA** — the principal **inhibitory** transmitter; target of alcohol and benzodiazepines.
- ★ **Glutamate** — the principal **excitatory** transmitter; NMDA receptors mediate **LTP**.
  *Split [N8]: inhibition vs excitation; if the stem mentions LTP or learning at the synapse, it is glutamate.*
- ★ **Endorphins** — endogenous opioids that **suppress** pain and produce euphoria.
- **Substance P** — **transmits** pain signals.
  *Split [N9]: same system, opposite direction — an R4 trap.*
- **Epinephrine** — adrenal medulla; the fast, acute stress hormone.
- **Cortisol** — adrenal cortex; the slow, sustained stress hormone; immunosuppressive and catabolic when chronic.
- **Oxytocin** — bonding, trust, labor, lactation.
- **Melatonin** — pineal; circadian sleep signal, released in darkness.
- **Testosterone / estrogen** — sex drive, aggression, secondary sex characteristics, and organizing effects on brain development.
- **Thyroid hormones** — metabolic rate; hypothyroidism presents with depression-like symptoms (a classic biopsychosocial confound).
- **Insulin / glucagon / leptin / ghrelin** — glucose and appetite regulation; **ghrelin** signals hunger, **leptin** signals satiety.
  *Split [N15]: match the hormone to the timescale and function the stem describes — acute stress, chronic stress, bonding, sleep, hunger.*
- ★ **Nervous vs endocrine signaling** — fast, electrical, targeted, brief vs slow, chemical, diffuse via bloodstream, sustained.
  *Split [N14]: if the stem emphasizes duration or whole-body effect, the answer is endocrine.*

## 21. Genes, development, and nervous-system disorders
- **Behavioral genetics** — the study of genetic contributions to behavior; twin and adoption designs.
- **Heritability** — the proportion of *variance in a population* attributable to genetic variance; **not** the proportion of an individual's trait that is genetic, and not transferable between populations. This distinction is the standard trap.
- **Gene–environment interaction** — genotype changes sensitivity to environment; **gene–environment correlation** — genotype changes which environments are encountered.
- **Temperament** — biologically based early reactivity; see [G30].
- **Adaptive value of traits and behaviors** — a trait persists because it raised reproductive fitness in the ancestral environment.
- **Regulatory genes and behavior** — BIO-tagged; gene expression, not sequence, as the proximate control of behavioral development.
- **Prenatal development** — zygote (0–2 wk) → embryo (3–8 wk, organogenesis, maximal teratogen sensitivity) → fetus (9 wk–birth).
- **Teratogen** — an agent producing prenatal harm (alcohol → fetal alcohol syndrome; the outline's implicit case).
- **Motor development** — reflexes (rooting, sucking, Moro, Babinski, grasping) → cephalocaudal and proximodistal progression → sit, crawl, walk.
- **Developmental changes in adolescence** — puberty, prefrontal maturation lagging limbic maturation (the reward/control gap underlying risk-taking), identity formation.
- ★ **Schizophrenia** — dopamine hyperactivity (mesolimbic) plus glutamate hypofunction; enlarged ventricles, reduced prefrontal and temporal volume.
- ★ **Depression** — monoamine deficiency (serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine); hyperactive HPA axis, reduced hippocampal volume.
- ★ **Alzheimer's disease** — beta-amyloid plaques, tau tangles, acetylcholine loss; hippocampal then cortical atrophy.
- ★ **Parkinson's disease** — degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the **substantia nigra**; resting tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity, postural instability.
  *Split [N18 / G28]: **schizophrenia and Parkinson's are both dopamine, in opposite directions** — excess mesolimbic signaling vs nigrostriatal loss. That is why antipsychotics cause parkinsonian side effects and L-DOPA can cause psychosis. An R4 trap AAMC can build directly.*
  *Split [G29]: Alzheimer's is cortical/hippocampal with memory first; Parkinson's is subcortical with motor signs first.*
- **Stem cell therapy to regenerate CNS neurons** — BIO-tagged; the outline's own named example of a biological intervention for nervous-system disorders.
- **Mirror neurons** — fire both when performing and when observing an action; the outline's named substrate for observational learning and vicarious emotion.
  *Split [N16]: a specific observation-linked neuron type vs plasticity, the general capacity for change.*

# PART IV — RESEARCH METHODS AND STATISTICS

*A separate closed deck of ~30 terms carrying ~12 questions (Skills 3 and 4 = 20% of the section). Two of AAMC's four published P/S sample items live here. This deck is the reason a vocabulary-only preparer caps at ~128 — but the deck itself is vocabulary, just a different lexicon.*

## 22. Study design
- ★ **Independent variable** — what the researchers **manipulated or assigned**.
- ★ **Dependent variable** — what the researchers **measured**.
  *Split [M1]: AAMC's published Skill 3 sample item is exactly this, and its distractor offers the **held-constant task type** as an IV. Ask "what did they set?" then "what did they record?"*
- ★ **Control variable** — deliberately held constant across conditions.
- ★ **Confounding variable** — uncontrolled, correlated with **both** IV and DV, offering an alternative explanation.
  *Split [M2]: intentionally fixed vs accidentally varying with everything.*
- ★ **Confounder** — causes both X and Y, making their association spurious.
- ★ **Mediator** — the **mechanism through which** X causes Y (X → M → Y).
- ★ **Moderator** — changes the **strength or direction** of the X→Y relation.
  *Split [M3]: third variable causing both · the intermediate step · the condition under which it holds. **The classic distractor swaps mediator for confounder** — a mediator is *part of* the causal chain, a confounder is *outside* it. AAMC names confound identification as its own P/S item type.*
- ★ **Random sampling** — who gets **into** the study; determines **external validity / generalizability**.
- ★ **Random assignment** — who goes into **which condition**; determines **internal validity / causal inference**.
  *Split [M4]: **the most valuable methods pair on the section.** Only random assignment of a *manipulated* variable licenses a causal claim. Random sampling with no assignment gives a generalizable correlation, not a cause.*
- ★ **Internal validity** — confidence that the IV actually caused the DV **in this study**.
- ★ **External validity** — confidence that the finding **generalizes** beyond this sample and setting.
  *Split [M5]: within vs beyond. Tightening control raises internal validity and often lowers external validity — that trade-off is itself an item.*
- ★ **Reliability** — consistency: the measure gives the same result on repetition.
- ★ **Validity** — accuracy: the measure captures what it claims to.
  *Split [M6]: **a measure can be reliable and invalid (a scale 5 lb heavy every time), but never valid without being reliable.** AAMC names both in one item type.*
- **Test–retest reliability** — consistency across time. **Inter-rater reliability** — consistency across observers. **Internal consistency** — consistency across items within a measure.
  *Split [M7]: which source of variation is being checked.*
- **Construct validity** — measures the abstract concept. **Content validity** — covers the whole domain. **Criterion validity** — correlates with an outcome (**predictive** = future, **concurrent** = now).
  *Split [M8]: an R2 set.*
- ★ **Experiment** — manipulation of an IV plus **random assignment**; supports causal claims.
- ★ **Quasi-experiment** — manipulation without random assignment, or comparison of pre-existing groups; causal claims are weakened by selection.
- **Correlational study** — measures variables as they occur; no manipulation, no causal claim.
  *Split [M14]: is there random assignment?*
- ★ **Cross-sectional** — many people, **one time point**; fast, but cannot establish temporal order and confounds age with cohort.
- ★ **Longitudinal** — the **same people over time**; establishes temporal order, but is slow and suffers attrition.
- ★ **Cohort study** — a defined group followed forward from **exposure to outcome**.
- **Case-control study** — starts from the **outcome** and looks backward at exposure; efficient for rare outcomes.
  *Split [M12/M13]: one snapshot · repeated measurement of the same people · forward from exposure · backward from outcome. AAMC explicitly names "the kinds of claims that can be made when using longitudinal, cross-sectional, or experimental data."*
- ★ **Qualitative research** — meaning, depth, non-numeric data, small n, generates hypotheses.
- ★ **Quantitative research** — measurement, counts, statistics, larger n, tests hypotheses.
  *Split [M15]: AAMC names a cross-study comparison item (qualitative results compared with quantitative) explicitly.*
- ★ **Ethnography** — immersive field observation of a group in its own setting.
- ★ **Survey** — structured self-report administered at scale.
- **Content analysis** — systematic coding of existing texts, media, or records.
- **Case study** — one unit examined in depth; rich but not generalizable.
  *Split [M16]: AAMC names "survey methods, ethnographic methods, experimental methods" directly.*
- **Participant observation** — the researcher joins the group being studied.
- **Naturalistic observation** — the researcher observes without intervening.
  *Split [M17]: is the researcher inside the situation?*
- ★ **Self-report measure** — what people say about themselves; vulnerable to social desirability and limited introspective access.
- ★ **Behavioral measure** — what people actually do.
- ★ **Implicit measure** — reaction-time-based (IAT); captures associations the person cannot or will not report.
  *Split [M18]: AAMC names both "implications of relying on self-report" and "the most appropriate way to assess prejudice in a study on implicit bias." A study finding an effect on an implicit measure but not a questionnaire is telling you the construct is unconscious or socially undesirable to report.*
- ★ **Social desirability bias** — answering to look good.
- ★ **Demand characteristics** — the participant guesses the hypothesis and behaves accordingly.
- ★ **Hawthorne effect** — behavior changes simply because one knows one is being observed.
  *Split [M19]: wanting to look good · trying to help the study · reacting to observation itself. A classic R2 set.*
- **Placebo effect** — improvement from the expectation of treatment; controlled by blinding.
- **Single-blind vs double-blind** — participant unaware vs participant **and** experimenter unaware; double-blind additionally controls experimenter expectancy.
- **Experimenter bias / expectancy effect** — the researcher's expectation influencing measurement or participant behavior.
- ★ **Selection bias** — non-random differences in **who entered** the study.
- **Attrition (mortality) bias** — non-random differences in **who dropped out**.
  *Split [M20]: entry vs exit.*
- **Response bias** — systematic distortion in **how respondents answer** (acquiescence, extreme responding).
- **Non-response bias** — systematic differences in **who declines to answer at all**.
  *Split [M21]: how they answer vs whether they answer.*
- **Convenience sample** — participants selected for accessibility; the standard threat to generalizability in a P/S passage.
- ★ **Operationalization** — how an abstract construct was **actually measured in this study** (locus of control → agreement with "luck plays a major role").
- **Conceptual definition** — what the construct means abstractly.
  *Split [M30]: the measurement vs the meaning. AAMC writes items asking which operationalization is appropriate for a named construct — this is the most common Skill 3 shape after IV/DV.*

## 23. Research ethics
- ★ **Informed consent** — obtained **before** participation: purpose, procedures, risks, and the right to withdraw.
- ★ **Right to withdraw** — available **throughout**, without penalty.
- ★ **Debriefing** — provided **after**, especially when deception was used; explains the true purpose and undoes any harm.
  *Split [M23]: before · throughout · after. AAMC names research-ethics identification as a P/S item type explicitly.*
- ★ **Deception** — withholding or misstating the study's purpose; **permitted** when necessary, risks are minimal, and debriefing follows.
- ★ **Coercion** — pressure that undermines voluntariness (excessive payment, authority over the participant); **never permitted**.
  *Split [M24]: deception is conditionally allowed; coercion never is. That asymmetry is the item.*
- ★ **Anonymity** — the researcher **cannot** link data to an identity.
- ★ **Confidentiality** — the researcher **can** link data to an identity but will not disclose it.
  *Split [M22]: an ethics-item staple. Anonymity is stronger; a study that follows people over time cannot be anonymous.*
- **IRB (institutional review board)** — the body that reviews and approves human-subjects research.
- **Beneficence / non-maleficence / justice / respect for persons** — the Belmont-style principles behind risk–benefit review and fair subject selection.
- **Vulnerable populations** — children, prisoners, cognitively impaired adults; require additional protections and assent where consent is not possible.

## 24. Statistics and data reasoning
- ★ **Positive correlation** — variables move **together**.
- ★ **Negative correlation** — variables move in **opposite** directions.
  *Split [M10]: **AAMC's published Skill 4 sample item is a pure sign item with no data in it.** The safest procedure: restate the effect as a directional sentence ("more bystanders → longer delay") and then read off the sign. Note that "more of X → more delay" is a *positive* correlation even though the outcome is bad.*
- **Correlation coefficient (r)** — −1 to +1; **magnitude** is strength, **sign** is direction. r = 0 means no *linear* relation, not no relation.
- ★ **Correlation vs causation** — an association licenses a causal claim only when the design rules out reverse causation and third variables.
- ★ **Reverse causation** — Y causes X rather than X causing Y.
- ★ **Third variable** — Z causes both X and Y.
  *Split [M9/M11]: these three — chance, reverse causation, third variable — are the **standard three distractors** on any causal-inference item, and one of them is usually the key.*
- ★ **Mean / median / mode** — arithmetic average / middle value / most frequent value.
- ★ **Median under skew** — with a **right-skewed** distribution or outliers, the mean is dragged toward the tail, so the **median** is the better summary.
  *Split [M28]: AAMC names this verbatim — "why income data are usually reported using the median rather than the mean." Income is right-skewed. In a right (positive) skew: mode < median < mean.*
- **Range / variance / standard deviation** — spread measures; SD is in the original units.
- ★ **Standard deviation** — spread of the **observations**.
- ★ **Standard error of the mean** — precision of the **estimated mean**; shrinks as n grows (SD/√n).
  *Split [M27]: describing the data vs describing the estimate. Error bars that shrink with a bigger sample are standard errors.*
- **Normal distribution** — symmetric; ~68% within 1 SD, ~95% within 2 SD, ~99.7% within 3 SD.
- **Percentile** — the proportion of the distribution at or below a score.
- ★ **Type I error (α)** — **false positive**: rejecting a true null hypothesis; α is usually 0.05.
- ★ **Type II error (β)** — **false negative**: failing to reject a false null.
  *Split [M25]: I = seeing something that is not there; II = missing something that is. Lowering α raises the risk of Type II. **Power = 1 − β**, and it rises with sample size and effect size.*
- **Null hypothesis** — the statement of no effect or no difference; statistical tests attempt to reject it.
- ★ **p-value** — the probability of data at least this extreme **if the null were true**; not the probability that the hypothesis is true.
- ★ **Statistical significance** — the result is unlikely under the null (p < α).
- ★ **Effect size / practical significance** — how **large** the effect is, and whether it matters.
  *Split [M26]: with a large enough n, a trivial effect becomes statistically significant. "Significant but small" is a keyed answer shape.*
- **Confidence interval** — a range of plausible values for a parameter; if a 95% CI for a difference includes 0, the result is not significant at α = 0.05.
- ★ **Incidence** — **new** cases in a population over a period.
- ★ **Prevalence** — **all existing** cases at a point in time.
  *Split [M29]: prevalence ≈ incidence × duration, so a treatment that extends survival without preventing disease **raises** prevalence while leaving incidence unchanged. A social-epidemiology staple.*
- **Relative risk vs absolute risk** — the ratio of risks between groups vs the actual probability; a large relative risk on a tiny absolute risk is the standard misreading.
- **Linear, semilog, and log-log scales** — AAMC explicitly names these; a straight line on a semilog plot indicates exponential change.
- **Reading a figure** — identify the axes and units, the units of the DV, the direction of the effect, and which comparison the error bars or significance markers refer to, **before** looking at the options.

# PART VI — THE 20-MINUTE REVIEW

## Sociology (do these first; they are 30% of the section and the cheapest points)

3. **Differential association vs labeling vs strain** — learned from intimates · created by the reaction · caused by blocked means.
4. **Role conflict vs role strain vs role exit** — two roles fighting · one role fighting itself · leaving the role.
5. **Achieved vs ascribed status** — did you do anything to get it?
6. **Primary vs secondary group** — is the relationship the end, or the instrument?
7. **In-group vs reference group** — belong vs measure yourself against (membership not required).

10. **Health vs healthcare disparity** — unequal outcomes vs unequal care.

12. **Culture lag vs culture shock** — a society falling behind its technology vs a person entering an unfamiliar culture.
13. **Subculture vs counterculture** — distinct within vs opposed to.
14. **Assimilation vs multiculturalism** — absorbed vs preserved side by side. *(And Piagetian vs sociological assimilation is a real homonym.)*
15. **Transmission vs diffusion** — within a group across generations vs between groups.
16. **Medicalization vs sick role vs illness experience** — society defines it as illness · society's expectations of the patient · the patient's own experience.
17. **Power vs authority** — imposed vs legitimate. *(Traditional / charismatic / rational-legal.)*
18. **Class consciousness vs false consciousness** — seeing your position vs believing against your interest.
19. **Social reproduction vs meritocracy vs social mobility** — inherited · earned · moved.
20. **Intersectionality** — the trap answer is always the *additive* framing; the key is that overlap produces something qualitatively distinct.
21. **Folkways / mores / taboos** and **formal/informal × positive/negative sanctions** — rude · immoral · revolting; four sanction cells.
22. **Anomie vs alienation vs strain** — normlessness (society) · estrangement from labor (individual) · goals minus means (structure).
23. **Sex vs gender**, **racialization vs racial formation**, **push vs pull**, **crude vs total vs age-specific rate**, **gentrification vs urban renewal**, **Malthus vs demographic transition** — six one-line outline pairs, six near-free points.
24. **Iron law of oligarchy vs McDonaldization** — who ends up in charge vs what the process comes to look like.
25. **Front vs back stage**, **impression management**, **looking-glass self vs role-taking**, **self-fulfilling prophecy vs stereotype threat**, **stereotype/prejudice/discrimination** — think · feel · do.

## Psychology
26. **The reinforcement × punishment 2×2** — (add/remove) × (increase/decrease). "Negative" = removed, never bad. Reinforcement always ↑, punishment always ↓. **The most-missed distinction on the section.**
27. **The four schedules** — ratio or interval first, then fixed or variable. VR = highest and hardest to extinguish; FI = scalloped.
28. **Classical vs operant** — involuntary response, stimulus *before* vs voluntary behavior, consequence *after*.
29. **Extinction vs habituation vs sensory adaptation vs punishment** — learned association weakening · unlearned response declining centrally · receptor fatigue · a consequence delivered.
30. **Dishabituation vs spontaneous recovery** — a new stimulus vs the passage of time.
31. **James-Lange vs Cannon-Bard vs Schachter-Singer** — arousal → emotion · both at once · arousal + label.
32. **Proactive vs retroactive interference** — old blocks new vs new blocks old. **Decay vs interference** — a delay vs a competitor.
33. **Episodic vs semantic**, **explicit vs implicit**, **short-term vs working**, **primacy vs recency** memory.
34. **Availability vs representativeness** — ease of recall vs match to a prototype. **Confirmation bias vs belief perseverance** — selective search vs refusal to update.
35. **Assimilation vs accommodation**, and the four Piaget stages by their marker (object permanence · failed conservation · concrete logic · abstract hypotheticals).
36. **Self-concept vs self-esteem vs self-efficacy vs locus of control** — who I am · how I rate myself · can I do *this* · does anything I do matter.
37. **FAE vs actor-observer vs self-serving bias** — others → disposition · the asymmetry · protecting my own esteem.
38. **Conformity vs compliance vs obedience**, and **normative vs informational influence** — fitting in vs being right.
39. **Social facilitation vs inhibition vs loafing vs deindividuation** — audience helps easy tasks · hurts hard ones · pooled effort drops · restraint drops.
40. **Groupthink vs group polarization** — dissent suppressed vs an existing lean intensified.
41. **Drive reduction vs incentive vs arousal theory** — push · pull · optimal level. **Esteem vs self-actualization** is the Maslow trap.
42. **Cognitive dissonance vs self-perception**, **central vs peripheral route**, **foot-in-the-door vs door-in-the-face**.
43. **Positive vs negative symptoms of schizophrenia**, **conversion vs factitious vs malingering**, **somatic symptom vs illness anxiety**, **bipolar I vs II**.
44. **Weber's law (ΔI/I = k)** and the **signal-detection 2×2** (hit/miss/false alarm/correct rejection; d′ vs criterion).
45. **Amygdala vs hippocampus** — emotional salience vs forming new declarative memories. *(The most-used brain pair in AAMC-published P/S items.)*
46. **Schizophrenia vs Parkinson's** — same neurotransmitter, opposite direction.
47. **Broca's vs Wernicke's** — production/non-fluent/aware vs comprehension/fluent/unaware.

## Methods and statistics
48. **Random sampling vs random assignment** — generalizability vs causal inference. **Only assignment of a manipulated variable licenses a causal claim.**
49. **IV vs DV** — set vs measured. Watch for the held-constant task offered as an IV.
50. **Confounder vs mediator vs moderator** — outside the chain · inside the chain · changes the strength.
51. **Reliability vs validity** — reliable-and-invalid is possible; valid-and-unreliable is not.
52. **Internal vs external validity** — and the trade-off between them.
53. **Type I vs Type II error** — false positive vs false negative; power = 1 − β.
54. **Significance vs effect size** — unlikely under the null vs large enough to matter.
55. **Mean vs median under skew** — income is right-skewed, so report the median.
56. **Incidence vs prevalence** — new cases vs all existing; longer survival raises prevalence, not incidence.
57. **Self-report vs behavioral vs implicit measure** — an effect on the IAT but not the questionnaire means the construct is unconscious or undesirable to admit.
58. **Anonymity vs confidentiality**, **deception vs coercion** (deception is conditionally allowed; coercion never is), **consent → withdraw → debrief** (before, throughout, after).
59. **Cross-sectional vs longitudinal** — cross-sectional cannot establish temporal order and confounds age with cohort.
60. **Positive vs negative correlation** — restate the effect as a directional sentence, then read off the sign. "More X → longer delay" is *positive*, even though the outcome is bad.
