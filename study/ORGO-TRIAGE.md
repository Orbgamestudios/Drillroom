**Three caveats where "not named" ≠ "safe to be blank on":**

1. **Grignard.** Not named. But "Nucleophilic addition reactions at C=O bond" *is* named, and an organometallic is the archetype of that. ## 2.2 ★ The one ranking that carries the most questions

**Nucleophilic acyl substitution reactivity, most → least reactive:**

```
acid halide  >  anhydride  >  [aldehyde > ketone]  >  ester ≈ carboxylic acid  >  amide  >  carboxylate
   (Cl⁻)        (RCOO⁻)                                (RO⁻)                      (NH₂⁻)     (dead)
```

**Two independent drivers, and they point the same way:**
1. **Leaving-group stability.** The weaker the base you kick out, the more reactive. Cl⁻ (conjugate base of a strong acid, super stable) leaves readily; ⁻NH₂ (pKa ~38) essentially never leaves.
2. **Resonance donation into the C=O.** The heteroatom lone pair pushes into the carbonyl, killing its electrophilicity. Donation strength: N > O > halogen (N is least electronegative, most willing to share). So the amide is the most stabilized and the least reactive.

**The decision rule this generates:** you can always convert **downhill** (more reactive → less reactive) in one step. **Uphill requires activation** (SOCl₂, an anhydride, or a coupling agent like DCC). If a passage shows a direct amide → acid chloride conversion, something is wrong or a reagent is doing the work.

**The three consequences that get tested more than the ladder itself:**
- **Peptide bonds are absurdly stable.** Amide is the bottom of the ladder; amide hydrolysis needs strong acid/base plus heat. Uncatalyzed peptide-bond half-life is on the order of centuries. *This is the entire reason proteases exist and the reason protein structure is durable.*
- **β-lactams (AAMC names this: "Strain, e.g., β-lactams").** A 4-membered ring forces the amide nitrogen out of planarity, so it can't donate into the carbonyl. Resonance stabilization is destroyed → an abnormally reactive amide. Penicillin exploits this to acylate the transpeptidase active-site serine. **This is the highest-probability single fact in the acid-derivatives block** because AAMC printed the example.
- **Saponification is irreversible.** Ester + NaOH → carboxyl**ate** + alcohol. The product is deprotonated, sitting at the bottom of the ladder, resonance-delocalized. Nothing pulls it back. (Contrast: Fischer esterification, acid-catalyzed, fully reversible.)

## 2.3 Nomenclature — the exact depth required, and no more

AAMC lists "Nomenclature" as a subtopic under aldehydes/ketones, alcohols, carboxylic acids, acid derivatives, and carbohydrates. It never asks you to *name* a compound in a free-response sense — it is multiple choice. **You need recognition, not generation.**

**Do learn:**
- Suffix → group mapping (the table above). Given "2-oxopropanoic acid," you must produce pyruvate's skeleton.
- **Priority order for the principal suffix** (highest first): carboxylic acid > ester > amide > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > amine. Higher-priority group gets the suffix; everything else becomes a prefix. This is why a molecule with both an OH and a COOH is a "hydroxy-…-oic acid," never an "-ol."
- Chain numbering gives the principal group the **lowest** locant.
- **Common names you will simply see used without definition:** formic (C1), acetic (C2), propionic (C3), butyric (C4); acetone, formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, benzaldehyde, phenol, toluene, aniline, benzoic acid, oxalic, citric, lactic, pyruvic, succinate, fumarate, malate, α-ketoglutarate, oxaloacetate, palmitate (C16:0), oleate (C18:1 Δ9 cis).
- **Greek-letter positions:** α is the carbon *adjacent* to the carbonyl, β the next, and so on. Non-negotiable — "α-hydrogen," "β-keto acid," "α-amino acid," and "β-oxidation" all depend on it.

**Do not learn:** full IUPAC substituent alphabetization, complex multi-ring nomenclature, E/Z vs cis/trans naming edge cases, stereodescriptor punctuation rules. Zero return.

# 3. THE REACTIONS ACTUALLY TESTED — AS DECISION RULES

Scope check before you start: the only reaction families AAMC names are (a) nucleophilic addition at C=O, (b) enolate chemistry, (c) oxidation/reduction of alcohols and aldehydes, (d) SN1/SN2 **of alcohols specifically**, (e) nucleophilic acyl substitution among acid derivatives, (f) two named amino-acid syntheses, (g) glycoside hydrolysis, (h) saponification. That's it. Eight families.

## 3.1 The master decision rule for the whole subject

Almost every MCAT orgo reaction question resolves with this three-step read. Use it before recalling any named reaction.

```
STEP 1 — Find the electrophile.  Look for a carbon with a partial positive charge:
         a carbonyl carbon (C=O), or a carbon bearing a good leaving group.
STEP 2 — Find the nucleophile.   Look for a lone pair or a negative charge:
         O, N, S, or a carbanion/enolate.
STEP 3 — Ask: can the electrophile's carbon expel a leaving group?
         NO  leaving group  →  ADDITION.       (aldehydes, ketones)
         YES leaving group  →  SUBSTITUTION.   (esters, amides, acid halides)
```

That single fork — addition vs. substitution — separates the aldehyde/ketone block from the acid-derivatives block and explains why the two behave differently. If you learn one thing about mechanism, learn this.

## 3.2 SN1 / SN2 (and E1/E2 for completeness)

**Scope note first:** the outline names "Substitution reactions: SN1 or SN2" **only under Alcohols**. E1/E2 appear nowhere. So the realistic tested question is *"this alcohol was converted to a halide — with inversion, racemization, or retention?"* Budget accordingly: ~15 minutes, not two hours.

**The decision table:**

| Factor | → SN2 | → SN1 | → E2 | → E1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Substrate** | **1° (best), methyl**; 2° maybe | **3° (best)**, 2° allylic/benzylic | any, favors 3° | 3° |
| **3° substrate?** | never (too crowded) | yes | yes | yes |
| **1° substrate?** | yes | never (cation too unstable) | yes w/ bulky base | never |
| **Nucleophile/base** | strong Nu, weak base (I⁻, CN⁻, N₃⁻, RS⁻, HS⁻) | weak/neutral (H₂O, ROH) | strong **bulky** base (t-BuO⁻, LDA) | weak base |
| **Solvent** | **polar aprotic** (DMSO, DMF, acetone) | **polar protic** (H₂O, ROH) | either | polar protic |
| **Kinetics** | 2nd order, rate = k[sub][Nu] | 1st order, rate = k[sub] | 2nd order | 1st order |
| **Steps** | 1, concerted | 2, via carbocation | 1, concerted | 2, via carbocation |
| **Stereochemistry** | **inversion** (backside attack) | **racemization** (planar cation, attacked from both faces) | anti-periplanar | mixture |
| **Rearrangement?** | no | **yes — carbocations rearrange** | no | yes |

**Fastest heuristic:** *substrate first, nucleophile second.* 3° → SN1/E1. 1° → SN2/E2. 2° → let the nucleophile decide: strong nucleophile + polar aprotic = SN2; weak nucleophile + polar protic = SN1.

**The alcohol-specific point that is actually tested:**
OH is a *terrible* leaving group (you'd be expelling HO⁻, pKa 15.7). It must be activated first:
- **Protonate it** (HCl, HBr, H₂SO₄) → now the leaving group is H₂O. 3° goes SN1 (racemization); 1° goes SN2 (inversion).
- **Make a tosylate (TsO–) or mesylate (MsO–)** — AAMC names these explicitly. The sulfonate is an excellent leaving group. **Key stereochemical fact: tosylate formation does not break the C–O bond, so configuration at carbon is RETAINED during the tosylation.** Inversion then happens on the subsequent SN2. Most-tested detail in the whole SN section.
- **SOCl₂ or PBr₃** → alkyl halide directly.

## 3.3 Nucleophilic addition at C=O — the aldehyde/ketone core

**Reactivity rule:** **aldehyde > ketone.** Two reasons, both tested: (1) the ketone has two bulky alkyl groups blocking approach, (2) alkyl groups are electron-donating and partially neutralize the carbonyl carbon's δ+. Electron-**withdrawing** neighbors (halogens, extra carbonyls) make C=O *more* reactive.

| Nucleophile | Product | Trigger words in a passage | The one fact to keep |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 equiv ROH, H⁺** | **hemiacetal** (one OH + one OR on the same C) | sugar cyclizes; "hemiacetal" | Intramolecular version = **cyclic sugar** |
| **2 equiv ROH, H⁺** | **acetal** (two OR on the same C) + H₂O | protecting group; glycoside | **Acetals are inert to base and nucleophiles** → used to protect a carbonyl. Removed by aqueous acid. **A glycosidic bond is an acetal.** |
| **1° amine (RNH₂)** | **imine / Schiff base** (C=N–R) + H₂O | PLP, retinal, "Schiff base" | **Optimal pH ≈ 4–5** — need acid to protonate the C=O, but too much acid protonates the amine and kills the nucleophile. Classic bell-curve rate question. |
| **2° amine (R₂NH)** | **enamine** (C=C–NR₂) | — | No N–H left to lose, so it dehydrates toward carbon instead |
| **NaBH₄** | alcohol | "mild reduction" | **Reduces aldehydes and ketones ONLY.** Works in protic solvent (MeOH/H₂O). Will not touch esters, acids, or amides. |
| **LiAlH₄** | alcohol / amine | "strong reduction" | Reduces **everything**: aldehyde, ketone, ester, carboxylic acid, amide, nitrile. Anhydrous ether, then aqueous workup. |
| **HCN / NaCN + H⁺** | **cyanohydrin** (C with OH + CN) | "adds a carbon" | Extends the chain by one; the –CN can be hydrolyzed to –COOH. This is one half of the Strecker synthesis. |
| **H₂O** | geminal diol (hydrate) | formaldehyde, chloral | Usually unfavorable; favored when the carbonyl is very electrophilic |

**Reduction outcome rule:** aldehyde → **1°** alcohol. Ketone → **2°** alcohol. Ester/acid → **1°** alcohol (LiAlH₄ only).

**Oxidation of aldehydes (AAMC names it as its own subtopic):**
Aldehyde → carboxylic acid, easily, with almost any oxidant (KMnO₄, CrO₃/Jones, Ag₂O, even air).
**Ketones resist oxidation** — there is no H on the carbonyl carbon to remove.
- **Tollens' reagent** (Ag(NH₃)₂⁺) → **silver mirror**. Positive for aldehydes and for **reducing sugars**. Negative for ketones — with the exception of α-hydroxy ketones like fructose, which tautomerize to an aldose in base and test positive.
- **Benedict's / Fehling's** → brick-red Cu₂O precipitate. Same diagnostic.

## 3.4 ★ Enolate chemistry — the block that pays off in biochemistry

**Why the α-hydrogen is acidic:** removing it gives an enolate whose negative charge delocalizes onto the electronegative oxygen. That's the entire explanation.

| Compound | α-H pKa | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Alkane | ~50 | never deprotonated |
| **Ketone / aldehyde α-H** | **~20** | deprotonated by strong base (LDA, NaOEt) |
| Ester α-H | ~25 | slightly less acidic |
| **β-dicarbonyl** (flanked by two C=O) | **~9–11** | **deprotonated by ordinary bases** — as acidic as phenol |
| Carboxylic acid O–H | ~4–5 | for comparison |

**Keto–enol tautomerism.** Keto and enol are *constitutional isomers* (an α-H and a π bond move), **not resonance forms** — this distinction is a favorite distractor. Keto is favored at equilibrium; exceptions are phenol (aromaticity) and β-dicarbonyls (conjugation + intramolecular H-bond). Catalyzed by acid *or* base.
**Consequence AAMC names explicitly — "α-racemization":** the enol/enolate is planar at the α carbon, so a stereocenter there is destroyed and re-formed from either face. **Any stereocenter α to a carbonyl will racemize in acid or base.** That is the tested consequence.

**Aldol.** Enolate of one carbonyl attacks the C=O of another → **β-hydroxy aldehyde/ketone**. Add heat/base → dehydration → **α,β-unsaturated carbonyl** (that's the *condensation* step). Net: a new **C–C bond between the α-carbon of one partner and the carbonyl carbon of the other.**

**Retro-aldol** — reverse it: cleave the C–C bond **between the α and β carbons** relative to the carbonyl. *This is not an abstract exercise:* the aldolase step of glycolysis (fructose-1,6-bisphosphate → DHAP + G3P) is a retro-aldol, and the aldolase step of gluconeogenesis is the forward aldol. If a B/B passage shows a C–C cleavage in a sugar metabolic pathway, retro-aldol is the answer.

**Kinetic vs. thermodynamic enolate** (AAMC names both):

| | Kinetic enolate | Thermodynamic enolate |
|---|---|---|
| Which one | **less** substituted | **more** substituted |
| Base | bulky, strong: **LDA** | small, weaker: NaOEt, NaOH |
| Temperature | low (−78 °C) | higher (RT+) |
| Reversible? | no — irreversible deprotonation | yes — equilibrating |
| Why | the less hindered α-H is reached **faster** | the more substituted enolate is more **stable** (like Zaitsev) |

Mnemonic: **kinetic = fast = least hindered = cold + bulky base.**

## 3.5 Carboxylic acids and their derivatives

**Acidity — the ladder you must be able to order cold:**

| Compound | pKa | Driver |
|---|---|---|
| Sulfonic acid (RSO₃H) | ~ −1 | three oxygens |
| **Carboxylic acid** | **~4–5** | resonance over two equivalent oxygens |
| **Phenol** | **~10** | phenoxide delocalizes into the ring |
| Thiol (RSH) | ~10 | large, polarizable S |
| Water | 15.7 | — |
| **Alcohol** | **~16–18** | no stabilization of RO⁻ |
| Amide N–H | ~17 | — |
| α-H of a ketone | ~20 | enolate resonance |
| Terminal alkyne | ~25 | sp carbon |
| Amine N–H | ~38 | — |
| Alkane | ~50 | — |

**Substituent effect (inductive):** electron-withdrawing groups near the COOH stabilize the carboxylate → **lower pKa = stronger acid.** Effect falls off sharply with distance. So: trichloroacetic (0.7) < dichloroacetic (1.3) < chloroacetic (2.9) < acetic (4.8). And 2-chlorobutanoic is a stronger acid than 4-chlorobutanoic.

**Reactions AAMC names:**

| Reaction | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Esterification (Fischer)** | acid + alcohol + H⁺ ⇌ ester + H₂O. **Reversible** — drive by excess alcohol or by removing water |
| **Amide formation** | acid + amine needs heat or a coupling agent (DCC). At room temp you just get an ammonium carboxylate salt |
| **Anhydride formation** | 2 acids − H₂O (heat) |
| **Reduction** | **LiAlH₄ → 1° alcohol. NaBH₄ does NOT reduce carboxylic acids** — the single most-tested reagent discrimination |
| **Decarboxylation** | see below |
| **Transesterification** | ester + a different alcohol + acid/base cat. → new ester. Reversible; drive with excess of the incoming alcohol |
| **Amide hydrolysis** | strong acid or base **plus heat**. Slow — bottom of the reactivity ladder |
| **Saponification** | ester + NaOH → carboxylate + alcohol. **Irreversible.** Triacylglycerol + NaOH → 3 soaps + glycerol |

**★ Decarboxylation — the rule, because it recurs constantly in metabolism:**
> A carboxylic acid loses CO₂ on heating **if there is a carbonyl (or equivalent electron sink) at the β position.**

Mechanism in one line: a six-membered cyclic transition state pushes the C–C bond's electrons onto the β-carbonyl oxygen, CO₂ leaves, an **enol** forms, and it tautomerizes to the ketone.
Applies to **β-keto acids** and **malonic acids (1,3-diacids)**. Does *not* apply to a plain carboxylic acid (acetic acid does not decarboxylate on heating).
Biological instances: acetoacetate → acetone (ketone bodies); the isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-KG dehydrogenase steps of the TCA cycle; the fatty-acid synthase malonyl-CoA step; PLP-dependent amino-acid decarboxylation to biogenic amines.

## 3.6 The two amino-acid syntheses AAMC names by name

These two are printed explicitly in the outline ("Synthesis of α-amino acids (OC): Strecker Synthesis, Gabriel Synthesis"), which makes them unusually likely for their size. Learn them as flowcharts; ignore the mechanism.

**Strecker synthesis** → an α-amino acid from an aldehyde:
```
R–CHO  +  NH₃   →  imine (R–CH=NH)
       +  HCN   →  α-aminonitrile (R–CH(NH₂)–C≡N)
       +  H₃O⁺  →  α-amino acid (R–CH(NH₂)–COOH)
```
Reading: an imine forms, cyanide adds (a cyanohydrin-type addition, but to C=N), the nitrile hydrolyzes to the acid. **Product is racemic** — no chiral influence anywhere.

**Gabriel synthesis** → a clean **primary** amine (and, in the malonic-ester variant, an amino acid):
```
phthalimide (N–H, acidic — flanked by two C=O)  + base  →  phthalimide anion
      + R–X   →  N-alkylphthalimide     [this is an SN2 — so use a 1° or 2° halide, never 3°]
      + hydrazine or aqueous acid/base  →  R–NH₂  +  phthalate
```
**The point of the reaction** (this is what gets asked, not the steps): direct alkylation of NH₃ with R–X **overalkylates** — you get a mixture of 1°, 2°, 3° amines and quaternary salt, because each product is a better nucleophile than the last. Gabriel caps the nitrogen with two carbonyls so it can only be alkylated once, then uncaps it. **Delivers exclusively the primary amine.**

## 3.7 Protecting groups — the concept, since AAMC names "Protection of alcohols"

The general logic (worth one minute, transfers everywhere): mask a reactive group → do chemistry elsewhere → unmask. Requirements are that the protection is high-yield, the mask is inert to the intervening conditions, and removal is selective.
- **Alcohol** → silyl ether (TMS-Cl, TBDMS-Cl); removed by fluoride (TBAF) or aqueous acid.
- **Ketone/aldehyde** → **acetal** (ethylene glycol, H⁺); inert to base, nucleophiles, and hydride; removed by aqueous acid. *The reason this matters: it lets you reduce an ester with LiAlH₄ while a ketone elsewhere in the molecule survives.*
- **Amine** → Boc or Fmoc — this is the basis of solid-phase peptide synthesis.

# 4. ★★ SPECTROSCOPY — THE HIGHEST-YIELD-PER-HOUR SECTION

AAMC prints this as a standalone topic block, **Molecular Structure and Absorption Spectra (OC)**, inside content category 4D. Its subtopics, verbatim: *infrared region — intramolecular vibrations and rotations; recognizing common characteristic group absorptions, fingerprint region; ultraviolet region — π-electron and nonbonding electron transitions, conjugated systems; NMR spectroscopy — protons in a magnetic field, equivalent protons, spin-spin splitting.*

Note what that list contains: **recognizing absorptions**, **equivalent protons**, **splitting**. It is a recognition task with a fixed answer key. **Memorize the two tables below and you own this topic.** No mechanism, no derivation, no exceptions to reason through. This is the single best trade in the entire orgo budget.

## 4.1 ★ INFRARED — the peak table

Units are wavenumbers (cm⁻¹). Read an IR spectrum right-to-left in your head as "does the molecule have an O–H, an N–H, a C=O?" — three questions answer most items.

| Peak (cm⁻¹) | Bond | Shape / intensity | The tell |
|---|---|---|---|
| **3200–3600** | **O–H (alcohol)** | **broad**, strong, rounded | The classic "big U." Broad because of H-bonding |
| **2500–3300** | **O–H (carboxylic acid)** | **very broad**, ugly, overlaps the C–H region | Wider and lower than an alcohol. **COOH = broad O–H + C=O at ~1710** |
| 3100–3500 | N–H (amine, amide) | medium, sharper than O–H | **1° amine = TWO peaks** (sym + antisym stretch); **2° amine = ONE peak**; 3° amine = none |
| ~3300 | ≡C–H (terminal alkyne) | sharp, narrow spike | Sharp spike sitting on top of the O–H region |
| 3000–3100 | =C–H (sp², vinyl/aryl) | medium | Just **above** 3000 |
| **2850–3000** | **C–H (sp³ alkyl)** | strong | Just **below** 3000. Present in nearly everything — low information |
| **2700 and 2800** | **aldehyde C–H** | two weak bands | **The "aldehyde doublet."** Diagnostic: distinguishes an aldehyde from a ketone when both show C=O ~1710 |
| 2220–2260 | **C≡N nitrile** | sharp, medium | Lone sharp peak in an otherwise empty region |
| 2100–2260 | C≡C alkyne | weak | Absent if the alkyne is symmetric (no dipole change) |
| **1650–1800** | **C=O carbonyl** | **strong, sharp, tall** | The most conspicuous peak in most spectra. Sub-ranges below |
| 1620–1680 | C=C alkene | weak/medium | — |
| 1450–1600 | aromatic C=C | medium, **several** bands | Two-to-four peaks in this window = ring |
| **below ~1500** | **fingerprint region** | dense, uninterpretable | AAMC names this. **Unique to each compound — used to confirm identity by matching against a reference, NOT to identify functional groups** |

**★ The C=O sub-table — the part most likely to be the actual question:**

| C=O position | Compound |
|---|---|
| ~1800 **and** ~1760 (**two peaks**) | **anhydride** — two coupled carbonyls, always a doublet |
| ~1800 | acid chloride |
| **~1735–1750** | **ester** |
| ~1725 | aldehyde |
| **~1710** | **ketone** |
| ~1710 (plus the very broad O–H) | carboxylic acid |
| **~1650** | **amide** — the lowest of the set |

**The ordering logic**, so you can reconstruct it if you blank: *more resonance donation into the C=O = weaker C=O bond = lower wavenumber.* N donates best → amide lowest. Cl donates worst (and withdraws inductively) → acid chloride highest. It's the reactivity ladder from §2.2 read backwards.

**Two shift rules that generate real questions:**
- **Conjugation LOWERS the C=O** by ~20–30 cm⁻¹ (partial single-bond character). An α,β-unsaturated ketone sits ~1680 rather than 1710.
- **Ring strain RAISES the C=O.** Cyclohexanone ~1715; cyclopentanone ~1745; cyclobutanone ~1780; **β-lactam ~1760** vs. a normal amide's 1650. *This is the same β-lactam fact from §2.2 arriving through a different door — the IR peak is direct evidence the amide resonance has been destroyed.*

**Hooke's-law reasoning (why the numbers are where they are):** frequency ∝ √(k/μ). **Stronger bond → higher wavenumber** (C≡C > C=C > C–C; C≡N > C=N > C–N). **Heavier atoms → lower wavenumber** (C–H ~3000 but C–D ~2200; C–H > C–O > C–Cl). If you're given an unfamiliar bond, reason from this instead of guessing. AAMC's subtopic "intramolecular vibrations" is pointing at exactly this.
One more consequence: a vibration is **IR-active only if it changes the dipole moment.** Perfectly symmetric stretches (N₂, O₂, symmetric alkynes) are IR-silent.

## 4.2 ★ ¹H-NMR — chemical shift table

**Scope note:** the outline says "**Protons** in a magnetic field." ¹³C NMR appears nowhere. Learn ¹H only.

Reference is **TMS at δ = 0.** Downfield = left = higher δ = **deshielded** = near electronegative atoms or π systems.

| δ (ppm) | Proton environment |
|---|---|
| **0.9** | CH₃ (alkyl) |
| **1.3** | CH₂ (alkyl) |
| 1.5 | CH (alkyl) |
| 1.6–2.6 | allylic (H on C next to C=C) |
| **2.0–2.5** | **α to a carbonyl** (H on the C adjacent to C=O) |
| 2.2–2.9 | benzylic (H on C next to a ring) |
| 2.2–3.0 | H on C–N (amine) |
| ~2.5 | terminal alkyne ≡C–H |
| 3.2–3.5 | H on C–halide (C–I ~3.2, C–Cl ~3.4, C–Br ~3.5) |
| **3.3–4.0** | **H on C–O** (alcohol, ether) |
| ~4.1 | H on the **O**-side CH₂ of an ester (O–CH₂–) |
| **4.6–6.0** | **vinyl** (H on C=C) |
| **6.5–8.5** (benzene itself 7.3) | **aromatic** |
| **9–10** | **aldehyde H (–CHO)** |
| **10.5–12** | **carboxylic acid H (–COOH)** |
| 0.5–5, variable | **O–H (alcohol) and N–H (amine)** |
| 5–8, broad | amide N–H |

**The four values worth locking absolutely:** alkyl **~1**, next-to-carbonyl **~2**, next-to-oxygen **~3.5**, aromatic **~7**, aldehyde **~9.5**, acid **~11**. Almost every NMR item is decided by one of these.

**Why the shift moves at all:** electronegative neighbors pull electron density off the proton → less local shielding from the applied field → the proton feels more field → **deshielded, shifts downfield.** More electronegative and closer = bigger shift. Aromatic protons are anomalously far downfield because the ring current from the circulating π electrons reinforces the applied field at the ring's rim.

## 4.3 ★ The four things an ¹H-NMR spectrum tells you

Read every spectrum in this fixed order. It converts a picture into a structure mechanically.

**1. NUMBER OF SIGNALS = number of chemically distinct proton environments.**
Equivalent protons (related by symmetry — the three H's of a methyl, the two ortho H's of a para-disubstituted ring) give **one** signal. AAMC names "equivalent protons" as a subtopic, so symmetry-counting is explicitly fair game.
Worked check: *p*-xylene shows **2** signals (4 aromatic H + 6 methyl H). Ethanol shows **3** (CH₃, CH₂, OH). Acetone shows **1** (all six H's equivalent). Propane shows **2**.

**2. CHEMICAL SHIFT (position) = what each proton is next to.** Use the table in §4.2.

**3. INTEGRATION (area under the peak) = the RELATIVE number of protons.**
It gives a **ratio, not an absolute count.** A 3:2 integration could be C₃H₅ or C₆H₄ — you resolve it against the molecular formula. Reported as a step height or a printed number.

**4. ★ SPLITTING (multiplicity) = how many protons are on the ADJACENT carbon.**

> **The n+1 rule: a signal is split into (n+1) peaks, where n is the number of protons on neighboring carbons that are NOT equivalent to it.**

| Neighbors (n) | Peaks | Name | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | singlet | 1 |
| 1 | 2 | doublet | 1:1 |
| 2 | 3 | triplet | 1:2:1 |
| 3 | **4** | quartet | 1:3:3:1 |
| 4 | 5 | quintet | 1:4:6:4:1 |
| 6 (two CH₃'s) | 7 | septet | — |

**The four splitting rules that generate the distractors:**
- **Equivalent protons do not split each other.** The three H's of a CH₃ are mutually equivalent → no self-splitting. Benzene is a singlet.
- **Coupling is essentially only to the adjacent carbon** (3 bonds). Protons two carbons away don't count at MCAT level.
- **★ O–H and N–H protons usually do NOT split and are not split** — they exchange rapidly with solvent, which averages the coupling away. They show up as **broad singlets** at variable shift. *Diagnostic trick that gets tested: add D₂O and the O–H/N–H signal disappears* (H swaps for D, which is invisible in ¹H-NMR). So in ethanol the CH₂ is a quartet from the CH₃, **not** a quintet from CH₃ + OH.
- **Count neighbors from each side separately if they differ.** In CH₃–CH₂–CH₃, the central CH₂ has 6 equivalent neighbors → septet.

**The pattern that should be instant:** an **ethyl group (CH₃CH₂–)** always gives a **triplet (3H, ~1.2) + quartet (2H, ~2–4)**. An **isopropyl** gives a **doublet (6H) + septet (1H)**. A **para-disubstituted benzene** gives **two doublets** in the aromatic region. Seeing any of these in the answer choices resolves the item on sight.

## 4.4 UV-Vis — the small amount required

AAMC's subtopics: π-electron and nonbonding electron transitions, conjugated systems; and (tagged GC) absorption in the visible gives the complementary color.

- Two transitions: **π → π\*** (strong) and **n → π\*** (weak, from a lone pair — requires a heteroatom like the carbonyl O).
- **★ The one rule: more conjugation → smaller HOMO–LUMO gap → absorbs LONGER wavelength (lower energy) → red shift.** An isolated C=C absorbs ~180 nm (deep UV, invisible on a standard instrument); it takes roughly **eight or more conjugated double bonds** to push absorption into the visible.
- **Colored organic compound ⇒ extensively conjugated.** AAMC's own example is **β-carotene** (11 conjugated C=C, absorbs blue-green, appears orange). Same logic covers heme, retinal, chlorophyll, and pH indicators (the outline names "effect of structural changes on absorption, e.g., indicators" — protonation changes the conjugation, changes λmax, changes the color; that's how an indicator works).
- **Complementary color:** you see what is *not* absorbed. Absorbs blue-violet → looks yellow-orange. Absorbs green → looks red/purple.
- Beer–Lambert (GC, but it shows up here): **A = εbc.** Absorbance is linear in concentration — that is the entire basis of the spectrophotometric assays in biochem passages (NADH at 340 nm, protein at 280 nm from Trp/Tyr).

## 4.5 Mass spectrometry — correct depth, which is *shallow*

**Read the tag before you study this.** "Mass spectrometer / Mass spectroscopy" is listed in category **4E** under **Atomic Nucleus (PHY, GC)** — beside atomic weight, isotopes, and radioactive decay. It carries **no OC tag**. That placement sets the depth: mass-to-charge separation and isotope masses, not orgo fragmentation puzzles. **Cap your time here at 10 minutes.** Everything below the line is the useful remainder.

| Concept | Rule |
|---|---|
| **x-axis** | **m/z** (mass-to-charge). Since z is almost always 1, read it as mass |
| **Molecular ion (M⁺)** | The peak at the **highest m/z** (ignoring isotope peaks) = the **molecular weight** |
| **Base peak** | The **tallest** peak, normalized to 100%. The most stable/abundant fragment — *not* necessarily M⁺ |
| **★ Nitrogen rule** | An **odd** molecular ion mass ⇒ an **odd number of nitrogens** |
| **★ M+2 isotope peaks** | **Cl: M+2 at ~1/3 the height of M** (³⁵Cl:³⁷Cl ≈ 3:1). **Br: M+2 ≈ equal to M** (⁷⁹Br:⁸¹Br ≈ 1:1). S: M+2 ~4%. Absence of an M+2 ⇒ no Cl, no Br |
| **Physics of separation** (the PHY/GC part, and the likelier question) | Ions are accelerated through a potential and bent by a magnetic field. **Radius of curvature ∝ √(m/z)** — heavier ions deflect less. Lighter/more-charged ions bend more sharply |

Common neutral losses, if a fragment table appears: **15** (CH₃), **17** (OH), **18** (H₂O), **29** (CHO or C₂H₅), **31** (OCH₃), **45** (COOH). Do not memorize beyond this list.

## 4.6 The 30-second cross-technique triage

If a passage hands you a spectrum, this is the order of operations:

```
IR first  →  WHICH functional groups are present?
             (broad 3300 = OH; strong ~1700 = C=O; both = COOH)
MS next   →  WHAT is the molecular weight?  odd = has N.  M+2 = has Cl or Br.
NMR last  →  HOW are the pieces connected?
             signals = environments · shift = neighbors · integration = ratio · splitting = adjacent H count
UV        →  is it conjugated / colored?
```

# 5. ★★ SEPARATIONS AND PURIFICATIONS

**Why this is ★4:** content category **5C** is titled "Separation and purification methods" and contains essentially nothing but this one topic block (~2.6 items on its own), and the same techniques resurface in every B/B protein-purification passage. Zero mechanism. Every question reduces to one sentence: **what property is this technique sorting on?**

## 5.1 ★ The master table — the property each method separates on

| Technique | **Separates on** | Elutes / moves first | Killer detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Extraction** | **solubility + acid-base character** | — | The workhorse. See §5.2 |
| **Simple distillation** | **boiling point** | lowest BP | Requires **ΔBP > ~25 °C** and BP < 150 °C |
| **Fractional distillation** | boiling point | lowest BP | For **ΔBP < 25 °C**. The column gives many vaporization-condensation cycles |
| **Vacuum distillation** | boiling point | lowest BP | For high-BP (>150 °C) or **heat-sensitive** compounds — lowering pressure lowers BP |
| **Recrystallization** | **solubility differences vs. temperature** | — | Dissolve in **hot** solvent, cool **slowly**. Impurities stay dissolved in the mother liquor. Fast cooling traps impurities in the lattice |
| **TLC** | **polarity** (adsorption) | nonpolar travels farthest | Silica/alumina = **polar** stationary. Polar compound sticks → **low Rf** |
| **Column chromatography (normal phase)** | polarity | **nonpolar elutes first** | Same physics as TLC, preparative scale |
| **Reverse-phase HPLC** | polarity, **inverted** | **polar elutes first** | Nonpolar (C18) stationary phase. High pressure = better resolution |
| **Gas chromatography (GLC)** | **volatility** (BP) + stationary-phase affinity | **lowest BP elutes first** | Sample must be volatile and thermally stable |
| **★ Size-exclusion (gel filtration)** | **size** | **LARGE elutes FIRST** | **The most-tested counterintuitive fact on this list.** Large molecules can't enter the bead pores → shorter path → out first. Small molecules detour through the pores → retained |
| **Ion-exchange** | **charge** | the one that doesn't bind the resin | **Cation** exchanger = **negatively** charged resin, binds **cations**. Anion exchanger = positive resin, binds anions. Elute with a **salt gradient or pH change** |
| **Affinity chromatography** | **specific binding** | everything else first | Column bears an immobilized ligand/antibody/Ni²⁺ (His-tag). Elute with free competing ligand, or change pH. Highest specificity of any method |
| **Paper chromatography** | polarity | — | Cellulose (polar) stationary, like normal-phase TLC |
| **Gel electrophoresis** | **charge-to-mass ratio** in an E-field | — | See §5.4 |

## 5.2 ★ Extraction — the acid-base separation flowchart

This is the most-tested lab technique in orgo, because it's really an acid-base problem wearing a lab coat.

**Principle:** two immiscible layers (organic + aqueous). A neutral organic compound prefers the organic layer. **Convert it to an ion and it moves to the aqueous layer.** All of extraction is that one sentence.

```
MIXTURE in an organic solvent
│
├─ wash with NaHCO₃ (weak base, pKa of H₂CO₃ ≈ 6.4)
│     → deprotonates CARBOXYLIC ACIDS (pKa ~5) only.  Carboxylate → aqueous.
│     → does NOT touch phenols (pKa ~10).             ★ This is how you split COOH from phenol.
│
├─ wash with NaOH (strong base)
│     → deprotonates PHENOLS (pKa ~10) too.  Phenoxide → aqueous.
│
├─ wash with HCl (acid)
│     → protonates AMINES (basic).  Ammonium salt → aqueous.
│
└─ NEUTRAL compounds stay in the organic layer throughout.
```
Recover any of them by re-neutralizing the aqueous layer, which flips the compound back to neutral and precipitates it or lets you re-extract it.

**★ Which layer is on top?** Decided by density. Get this wrong and you throw away your product.
- **On top of water (less dense):** diethyl ether (0.71), hexane (0.66), ethyl acetate (0.90), toluene (0.87).
- **Below water (more dense):** **dichloromethane (1.33), chloroform (1.49)**, carbon tetrachloride (1.59).
- Mnemonic: **halogenated solvents sink.**

**Partition/distribution coefficient:** K = [solute]organic / [solute]aqueous. Practical rule that shows up as a quantitative item: **several small extractions recover more than one large extraction with the same total solvent volume.**

## 5.3 TLC arithmetic

**Rf = (distance traveled by the spot) / (distance traveled by the solvent front).** Always between 0 and 1; measure from the origin line.

- **High Rf** = traveled far = **nonpolar** (weak interaction with the polar plate).
- **Low Rf** = stuck near the origin = **polar**.
- More polar mobile phase → everything moves further → **all Rf values increase**.
- Uses: monitor a reaction (starting material spot disappears, product appears); check purity (one spot = pure); identify by co-spotting against a standard.
- **Rf is not a physical constant** — it depends on plate, solvent, and temperature, so comparisons are valid only within a single plate.

## 5.4 Electrophoresis — worth its own block because B/B uses it constantly

| Method | Separates on | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **SDS-PAGE** | **size only** | SDS denatures the protein and coats it with uniform negative charge, masking intrinsic charge. All proteins have the same charge-to-mass ratio → the gel sieves purely by size. **Smallest travels FARTHEST.** Add β-mercaptoethanol/DTT to also break disulfides and separate subunits |
| **Native PAGE** | size **and** charge **and** shape | Protein stays folded and functional. Harder to interpret; used when you need retained activity |
| **★ Isoelectric focusing (IEF)** | **pI** | A pH gradient is set up in the gel. Each protein migrates until it reaches the pH equal to its **pI**, where its net charge is zero, and then stops |
| **2D gel** | pI, then size | IEF in one dimension, then SDS-PAGE perpendicular to it |

**★ Migration direction rule (memorize, it also answers amino-acid items):**
> **pH < pI → protein is protonated → net POSITIVE → migrates to the CATHODE (−).**
> **pH > pI → protein is deprotonated → net NEGATIVE → migrates to the ANODE (+).**
> **pH = pI → no net charge → does not migrate; minimum solubility; tends to precipitate.**

Mnemonic: *low pH, positive charge; the molecule runs away from the acid.*

## 5.5 ★ Resolution of racemic mixtures — AAMC names this explicitly

The outline prints "**Racemic mixtures, separation of enantiomers (OC)**" as its own subtopic under 5C, with the note that you're responsible for it *only* at the orgo-course level.

**The problem:** enantiomers have **identical** boiling points, melting points, densities, solubilities, and Rf values in an achiral environment. Therefore:
> **Distillation, recrystallization, ordinary chromatography, and extraction ALL FAIL to separate enantiomers.** This is the answer to the "which technique cannot separate…" question.

**The three things that work — all of them work by introducing a second chiral element:**
1. **Diastereomeric salt formation (the classic).** React the racemic mixture with a single enantiomer of a chiral resolving agent — e.g., a racemic acid + (R)-1-phenylethylamine. The products, (R,R) and (S,R), are **diastereomers, not enantiomers**, so they have genuinely different solubilities and melting points. Separate by ordinary recrystallization, then regenerate the free enantiomers by neutralizing.
2. **Chiral stationary-phase chromatography.** The column itself is chiral, so the two enantiomers form transient diastereomeric complexes and elute at different times.
3. **Kinetic / enzymatic resolution.** An enzyme is chiral and consumes only one enantiomer, leaving the other behind.

**The generalization worth carrying:** *enantiomers only behave differently in a chiral environment.* That is also why one enantiomer of a drug is active and the other isn't — the receptor is chiral.

# 6. ★★ STEREOCHEMISTRY

Appears in the outline **four separate times**: 5B ("Stereochemistry of covalently bonded molecules"), 5C (racemic separation), 5D (amino acids — "absolute configuration at the α position"), and 5D again (carbohydrates — "absolute configuration," "epimers and anomers"). Nothing else in orgo is cross-listed this heavily.

AAMC's subtopics, verbatim: *structural isomers; stereoisomers (diastereomers, enantiomers, cis-trans); conformational isomers; polarization of light, specific rotation; absolute and relative configuration; conventions for writing R and S forms; conventions for writing E and Z forms.*

## 6.1 ★ The isomer decision tree — run this top-down

```
Two structures, same molecular formula.
│
├─ Different ATOM CONNECTIVITY?
│    └─ YES → STRUCTURAL (constitutional) ISOMERS.  Different compounds, different properties.
│             (e.g. n-butane vs isobutane; ethanol vs dimethyl ether)
│
└─ Same connectivity, different 3-D arrangement → STEREOISOMERS
     │
     ├─ Interconvert by ROTATING single bonds only?
     │    └─ YES → CONFORMATIONAL isomers (conformers).
     │             Not separable at room temperature. Same compound.
     │
     └─ Interconversion requires BREAKING a bond → CONFIGURATIONAL isomers
          │
          ├─ Are they NON-SUPERIMPOSABLE MIRROR IMAGES?
          │  (equivalently: is EVERY stereocenter inverted?)
          │    └─ YES → ENANTIOMERS
          │
          └─ NO (some but not all centers differ) → DIASTEREOMERS
                   includes cis/trans (E/Z) alkenes and ring isomers,
                   epimers, and anomers.
```

## 6.2 ★ Enantiomer vs. diastereomer — the property table that decides items

| | **Enantiomers** | **Diastereomers** |
|---|---|---|
| Relationship | mirror images, non-superimposable | not mirror images |
| Stereocenters | **ALL** inverted | **some but not all** differ |
| Melting point, boiling point, density, solubility | **IDENTICAL** | **DIFFERENT** |
| Rotation of plane-polarized light | equal magnitude, **opposite sign** | different, unrelated magnitudes |
| Separable by ordinary methods (distillation, recrystallization, normal chromatography) | **NO** | **YES** |
| Reactivity with **achiral** reagents | identical | different |
| Reactivity with **chiral** reagents (enzymes, receptors) | **different** | different |
| How many can there be? | exactly **one** partner | many |

**The one-sentence version:** *enantiomers differ only in a chiral environment; diastereomers differ everywhere.*

## 6.3 One-step identification procedures

**Is there a stereocenter?**
> An **sp³ carbon bearing four different groups.** Scan every sp³ carbon; if two substituents are identical, it isn't one. (Watch the two "different" groups that are only different further out — e.g. C3 of 3-methylhexane.)

**Is the molecule chiral?**
> **Does it have an internal mirror plane (or any improper axis)?** No internal symmetry → chiral. Yes → achiral. Do *not* just count stereocenters — see meso below.

**How many stereoisomers?**
> **Maximum 2ⁿ** for n stereocenters. **Fewer if meso forms exist** (internal symmetry collapses pairs). 2 centers → up to 4; 4 centers (glucose) → 16.

**★ Is it MESO?**
> **Has stereocenters, but also has an internal mirror plane → superimposable on its own mirror image → ACHIRAL → optically INACTIVE.**
> Test: draw the mirror image and try to superimpose, or look for a plane that cuts the molecule into two mirror halves.
> Canonical case: (2R,3S)-tartaric acid. The two halves are identical and opposite; the rotations cancel internally.
> **The trap:** "this molecule has two stereocenters, therefore it is optically active" is false whenever it's meso.

**★ Assigning R/S:**
```
1. RANK the four groups by CIP priority: higher atomic number = higher priority.
   Compare atom-by-atom outward from the stereocenter; stop at the FIRST POINT OF DIFFERENCE.
   Duplicate atoms for double/triple bonds:  C=O counts as (O, O, C);  C≡N counts as (N, N, N).
   Rough ranking:  I > Br > Cl > S > F > O > N > C > H
2. Put the LOWEST priority (usually H) pointing AWAY from you.
3. Trace 1 → 2 → 3.   Clockwise = R.   Counterclockwise = S.
4. If the lowest priority points TOWARD you: assign as drawn, then REVERSE the answer.
```
**★ Fischer projection shortcut** (essential for sugars and amino acids): in a Fischer, **horizontal lines come toward you (wedges); vertical lines go away.** So if the H is on a **horizontal** line, determine the direction as drawn and then **flip** the answer. If the H is **vertical** (pointing away already), read it directly.

**E vs Z (for alkenes, when "cis/trans" is ambiguous):**
> Rank the two substituents on **each** alkene carbon separately by CIP. Higher-priority groups on the **same** side = **Z** (*zusammen*, together). Opposite sides = **E** (*entgegen*). Mnemonic: **Z = Zame Zide.**
> "Cis/trans" is the older two-substituent-only language; E/Z is general.
> Note that cis/trans alkene isomers are **diastereomers**, and they don't interconvert without breaking the π bond.

## 6.4 Optical activity — the numbers

- **Chiral molecule → rotates plane-polarized light.** Achiral (including **meso**) → does not.
- **Dextrorotatory (+) or (d)** = clockwise. **Levorotatory (−) or (l)** = counterclockwise. Measured on a polarimeter.
- **★ (+)/(−) has NO fixed relationship to R/S, or to D/L.** Sign of rotation is an experimental measurement; R/S is a structural assignment. You cannot deduce one from the other. This is a standard distractor.
- **Racemic mixture** = exactly 50:50 of two enantiomers → **net rotation zero, optically inactive** — but it is *not* achiral and it *is* separable in principle (see §5.5). Written (±) or *dl*.
- **Specific rotation:** [α] = α_observed / (l × c), with **l** the path length in **decimeters** and **c** in g/mL. It is the normalized, reportable constant.
- **Enantiomeric excess:** ee = |%R − %S| = (observed [α] / pure [α]) × 100. A 75:25 mixture has 50% ee.

## 6.5 Absolute vs. relative configuration — and the D/L system

AAMC names "absolute and relative configuration" explicitly.
- **Absolute configuration** = the actual, real 3-D arrangement at a center. R/S is an absolute descriptor.
- **Relative configuration** = the arrangement stated relative to some other molecule or reference. **D/L is a relative system**, defined against (+)-glyceraldehyde.

**★ D/L rule (for sugars and amino acids, in Fischer projection):**
> Look at the stereocenter **furthest from the carbonyl / highest-numbered stereocenter**. **OH (or NH₂) on the RIGHT = D. On the LEFT = L.**

**The two biological facts that follow, and they get tested directly:**
- **Essentially all biological sugars are D.** D-glucose, D-ribose, D-fructose.
- **Essentially all proteinogenic amino acids are L**, and **all are (S) except cysteine, which is (R)** — not because its geometry differs, but because sulfur outranks the carboxyl carbon in CIP priority, which reshuffles the ranking. **Glycine is achiral** (two H's on the α carbon), so it has no descriptor at all.
- **D/L does not predict the sign of rotation.** D-fructose is levorotatory. This is the same trap as §6.4.

# 7. ★ BIOLOGICAL ORGO — WHERE THE B/B ITEMS LIVE

The 5% orgo in Bio/Biochem (2–4 items) comes almost entirely from here, and this material **double-dips with the 25% first-semester-biochem load in both sections.** Best cross-subject return in the document.

## 7.1 ★★ Amino acids

**Structure:** a central α-carbon carrying **–NH₃⁺, –COO⁻, –H, and the R group.** At physiological pH it is a **zwitterion** (dipolar ion — AAMC's own phrasing) — net charge zero but two formal charges present.

**★ The pKa table — memorize the seven ionizable side chains:**

| Group | pKa | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **α-COOH** | **~2** | deprotonated at any physiological pH |
| **α-NH₃⁺** | **~9–10** | protonated at physiological pH |
| Asp (D) side chain | 3.9 | acidic |
| Glu (E) side chain | 4.3 | acidic |
| **His (H) imidazole** | **6.0** | **★ the only side chain that buffers at physiological pH** — which is why His is the catalytic residue in so many enzymes (chymotrypsin, carbonic anhydrase) and the proton acceptor in hemoglobin's Bohr effect |
| Cys (C) thiol | 8.3 | — |
| Tyr (Y) phenol | 10.1 | — |
| Lys (K) | 10.5 | basic |
| Arg (R) guanidinium | 12.5 | most basic; **positively charged at every physiological pH** |

**★ Computing pI (isoelectric point) — the procedure:**
> **pI = the average of the two pKa values that flank the neutral (net-zero-charge) species.**
- **Neutral side chain:** pI = (pKa_COOH + pKa_NH₃)/2 ≈ (2 + 9.5)/2 ≈ **~5.8–6.0**
- **Acidic side chain (D, E):** average the **two LOWEST** pKa's → pI ≈ 3
- **Basic side chain (K, R, H):** average the **two HIGHEST** pKa's → pI ≈ 7.6 (H) to 10.8 (K, R)

**★ Charge rule (identical to the electrophoresis rule in §5.4 — learn it once, use it twice):**
> pH **below** pI → net **positive**. pH **above** pI → net **negative**. pH **=** pI → net zero, minimum solubility, precipitates, doesn't migrate.
Titration-curve reading: each pKa is the center of a **buffering plateau**; the **pI sits at the steep inflection between** two plateaus, not on a plateau.

**Classification (AAMC names "acidic or basic" and "hydrophilic or hydrophobic"):**

| Class | Members |
|---|---|
| **Nonpolar / hydrophobic** | Gly, Ala, Val, **Leu, Ile, Met**, Pro, Phe, Trp |
| **Polar uncharged** | Ser, Thr, Cys, Tyr, Asn, Gln |
| **Acidic (−)** | **Asp, Glu** |
| **Basic (+)** | **Lys, Arg, His** |
| **Aromatic** | Phe, Trp, Tyr (+His) — **absorb at 280 nm; that's how protein concentration is measured** |
| **Sulfur-containing** | Cys, Met |
| **Special geometry** | **Gly** (no side chain → achiral, maximum backbone flexibility); **Pro** (side chain bonded back to the backbone N → rigid, **helix breaker**) |

**★ Reactions AAMC names:**
- **Peptide bond** = an **amide**, formed by condensation (loses H₂O), between one residue's carboxyl and the next one's amino group. **N-terminus is written on the left.**
  - **The key structural consequence:** the nitrogen lone pair delocalizes into the carbonyl, giving the C–N bond **~40% double-bond character**. Therefore the peptide bond is **planar and cannot rotate**, and it is almost always **trans** (proline is the exception, ~10% cis). This rigidity is exactly what makes regular secondary structure possible — rotation is allowed only about the φ and ψ bonds flanking each α-carbon.
  - **Hydrolysis is very slow** (bottom of the acid-derivative ladder, §2.2) — requires strong acid/base and heat, or a protease.
- **Cysteine → cystine:** two thiols **oxidized** to a **disulfide (–S–S–)**. Stabilizes tertiary/quaternary structure; found in extracellular and secreted proteins. **Broken by reducing agents** (β-mercaptoethanol, DTT, TCEP) — which is why they're added to SDS-PAGE samples.
- **Synthesis (OC-tagged):** Strecker and Gabriel — see §3.6.

## 7.2 ★ Carbohydrates

AAMC's subtopics: nomenclature and classification, absolute configuration, cyclic structure and conformations of hexoses, epimers and anomers, hydrolysis of the glycoside linkage, keto-enol tautomerism.

**Classification:** aldose (aldehyde at C1) vs. **ketose** (ketone, usually at C2). Size: triose, tetrose, pentose, **hexose**. So glucose = an aldohexose; fructose = a ketohexose.

**Absolute configuration:** D if the OH on the **highest-numbered stereocenter** (C5 in a hexose) is on the **RIGHT** in a Fischer projection. Glucose has 4 stereocenters → 2⁴ = **16 aldohexoses**, 8 of them D.

**★ Cyclization — the single most important structural idea here:**
> The ring is an **intramolecular hemiacetal** (from an aldose) or **hemiketal** (from a ketose). An internal –OH attacks the carbonyl carbon.
- C5-OH attacks C1 → **six-membered pyranose** (glucose).
- C4-OH attacks C1 → **five-membered furanose**. Fructose forms a furanose.
- **Anomeric carbon** = the former carbonyl carbon. **Identify it as the ring carbon bonded to TWO oxygens** (the ring O and an OH/OR). That's the one-step ID.

**★ α vs. β:** in a Haworth projection of a D-sugar, **α = anomeric OH points DOWN** (trans to the C6 CH₂OH); **β = anomeric OH points UP** (cis to CH₂OH). β-D-glucopyranose is the most stable hexose because in the chair **every** substituent, including the anomeric OH, can sit **equatorial**.

**Relationships — the vocabulary that gets tested directly:**

| Term | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Epimer** | diastereomers differing at **exactly one** stereocenter | glucose/galactose = **C4** epimers; glucose/mannose = **C2** epimers |
| **Anomer** | epimers differing specifically at the **anomeric carbon** | α- vs. β-D-glucose |
| **Mutarotation** | α and β interconvert in water by reopening through the free aldehyde, until an equilibrium optical rotation is reached | glucose settles ~64% β, ~36% α |
| **Enantiomers** | D- vs. L- form of the same sugar | D- vs. L-glucose |

**★ Glycosidic bond = an ACETAL.** Formed by condensation of the anomeric OH with another alcohol; **cleaved by hydrolysis** (aqueous acid or a glycosidase). AAMC names "hydrolysis of the glycoside linkage."
- **α-1,4** — starch, glycogen (digestible by amylase). **α-1,6** — glycogen/amylopectin branch points.
- **β-1,4** — **cellulose. Humans lack the enzyme**; that's dietary fiber. Also lactose (gal-β1,4-glc).

**★ Reducing sugars:** a sugar is **reducing** if its anomeric carbon is **free** (not tied up in a glycosidic bond), so it can reopen to the aldehyde and be oxidized. Positive **Tollens'** (silver mirror) and **Benedict's** (brick-red Cu₂O).
- **Reducing:** all monosaccharides (including **fructose** — it tautomerizes to an aldose in base), maltose, lactose.
- **★ NOT reducing: sucrose** — both anomeric carbons are locked in the glc-α1↔2β-fru linkage. The standard exam answer.

**Keto-enol tautomerism (AAMC names it here specifically):** in base, glucose ⇌ **enediol** ⇌ fructose or mannose. Two payoffs: it's why fructose gives a false-positive Tollens', and it is the *chemical* content of the phosphoglucose isomerase step (G6P ⇌ F6P) in glycolysis.

**Disaccharides:** maltose = glc-α1,4-glc; lactose = gal-β1,4-glc; sucrose = glc-α1,2β-fru.
**Polysaccharides:** starch (amylose, linear α1,4; amylopectin, branched) and glycogen (same, more branched) = storage; cellulose (β1,4) and chitin = structural.

## 7.3 ★ Lipids

AAMC's subtopics: triacylglycerols; free fatty acids and **saponification**; phospholipids/phosphatids; sphingolipids; waxes; fat-soluble vitamins; steroids; prostaglandins.

| Lipid | Structure | Linkage | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Triacylglycerol** | glycerol + **3** fatty acids | **3 esters** | Storage. Most energy per gram — the carbons are fully reduced and it's stored anhydrous, unlike glycogen |
| **Free fatty acid** | long chain + terminal COOH | — | Fuel; amphipathic; the COOH is ionized at physiological pH |
| **Phospholipid** | glycerol + **2** fatty acids + phosphate + head group | 2 esters + phosphodiester | **Amphipathic → bilayer.** The whole basis of membranes |
| **Sphingolipid** | **sphingosine** backbone (not glycerol) | **amide** linkage to the fatty acid | Sphingomyelin — myelin sheath |
| **Wax** | long-chain fatty acid + long-chain alcohol | **1 ester** | Waterproofing |
| **Steroid** | **four fused rings, 6-6-6-5** | — | Cholesterol (membrane fluidity buffer), steroid hormones, bile acids, vitamin D |
| **Prostaglandin** | 20-carbon, from **arachidonic acid**, has a 5-membered ring | — | Paracrine/autocrine: inflammation, pain, fever, platelets. **COX is the NSAID target** |
| **Fat-soluble vitamins** | **A, D, E, K** | — | A = retinal/vision; D = Ca²⁺ absorption; E = membrane antioxidant; K = γ-carboxylation of clotting factors |

**★ Saponification** — the reaction AAMC names by name: **triacylglycerol + NaOH → 3 fatty-acid salts (soap) + glycerol.** It is **base hydrolysis of an ester** and it is **irreversible** (the carboxylate product is deprotonated and resonance-stabilized; nothing pulls it back). Soap works because it's amphipathic: nonpolar tails inward, charged heads outward → micelle that solubilizes grease.

**★ Melting-point rule** (recurs in every membrane-fluidity question):
> **Saturated** chains are straight, pack tightly, maximize van der Waals contact → **high MP, solid at RT** (animal fat).
> **Cis-unsaturated** chains have a **kink** at each double bond, pack badly → **low MP, liquid** (oils), and **increase membrane fluidity**.
> **Trans**-unsaturated chains have no kink and behave like saturated ones.
> **Longer chain → higher MP** (more surface for dispersion forces).
> **Cholesterol is a bidirectional buffer:** it *decreases* fluidity at high temperature (its rigid ring system restrains the chains) and *increases* it at low temperature (it wedges between chains and prevents tight packing).

## 7.4 Phenols, quinones, and biological aromatic heterocycles

AAMC prints "**Phenols (OC, BC)** — oxidation and reduction (e.g., hydroquinones, ubiquinones): biological 2e⁻ redox centers" and "**Polycyclic and Heterocyclic Aromatic Compounds (OC, BC)** — biological aromatic heterocycles." Note the framing: these are **recognition and redox** topics, not reaction-mechanism topics. There is **no electrophilic aromatic substitution anywhere in the outline.**

- **Phenol pKa ≈ 10** — far more acidic than an alcohol (~17) because the phenoxide's charge delocalizes into the ring. Electron-withdrawing ring substituents (nitro) push it lower still.
- **★ Hydroquinone ⇌ quinone** is a reversible **2-electron, 2-proton** couple. This is AAMC's named example of a "biological 2e⁻ redox center," and it is the chemistry of:
  - **Ubiquinone (CoQ)** in the electron transport chain — and its ability to carry *both* one and two electrons is exactly why it can accept from 2e⁻ carriers (NADH, FADH₂) and hand off to the 1e⁻ cytochromes.
  - **Vitamin K** (a naphthoquinone) and **vitamin E** (a chromanol) — the same motif.
- **Aromaticity criteria** (in case a structure question appears): cyclic, planar, fully conjugated, and **4n+2 π electrons** (Hückel). A lone pair can count toward the π system if it sits in a p orbital.
- **The heterocycles to recognize on sight** — they're all over B/B passages:

| Ring | Where it appears |
|---|---|
| **Imidazole** | histidine side chain (pKa 6.0) |
| **Indole** | tryptophan |
| **Phenol** | tyrosine |
| **Purine** (bicyclic) | adenine, guanine; also caffeine |
| **Pyrimidine** (single ring) | cytosine, thymine, uracil |
| **Porphyrin** | heme (with Fe²⁺), chlorophyll (Mg²⁺), cobalamin (Co) |
| **Isoalloxazine** | FAD / FMN |
| **Nicotinamide** (pyridine) | NAD⁺ / NADP⁺ |
| **Pterin** | folate / THF |
| **Thiazole** | thiamine (B₁) |

**Memory hook for the nucleobases:** **PUR**ines are **PUR**e **A**s **G**old (**A**denine, **G**uanine — bicyclic, larger). **PY**rimidines are **CUT** (**C**ytosine, **U**racil, **T**hymine — single ring, smaller).

# 9. LAST-LOOK PAGE

The facts with the highest ratio of test-appearance to memorization cost. Read this the morning of.

**Spectroscopy**
- IR: **broad 3200–3600 = O–H alcohol** · **very broad 2500–3300 + sharp 1710 = COOH** · **strong 1700–1750 = C=O** · **two weak peaks at 2700/2800 = aldehyde** · **two C=O peaks ~1800+1760 = anhydride** · **~1650 = amide (lowest)** · **below 1500 = fingerprint region (identity matching only)**
- Conjugation **lowers** C=O; ring strain **raises** it (β-lactam ~1760 vs. amide 1650)
- NMR shifts: **alkyl ~1 · α-to-carbonyl ~2 · on C–O ~3.5 · vinyl ~5 · aromatic ~7 · aldehyde ~9.5 · COOH ~11**
- **n+1 rule.** Equivalent H's don't split each other. **O–H/N–H = broad singlet, no splitting, vanishes on D₂O shake**
- Signals = environments · shift = neighbors · integration = ratio · splitting = adjacent H count
- More conjugation → longer λmax → colored (β-carotene)
- MS: **odd M⁺ ⇒ odd number of N** · **M+2 at 1/3 height = Cl** · **M+2 equal height = Br**

**Separations**
- **Size-exclusion: LARGE elutes FIRST**
- Normal-phase (TLC/silica): **polar sticks, low Rf**. Reverse-phase HPLC: **polar elutes first**
- **NaHCO₃ extracts carboxylic acids but NOT phenols** (the pKa 5 vs. 10 split)
- **Halogenated solvents (DCM, chloroform) sink below water**; ether/hexane/ethyl acetate float
- SDS-PAGE = **size only, smallest travels farthest**. IEF = stops at **pH = pI**
- **pH < pI → net positive → migrates to cathode.** pH > pI → negative → anode
- **Enantiomers cannot be separated by distillation, recrystallization, or ordinary chromatography.** Convert to diastereomers first

**Stereochemistry**
- Stereocenter = **sp³ C with four different groups**. Max **2ⁿ** stereoisomers
- **Enantiomers = ALL centers inverted, identical physical properties.** Diastereomers = some differ, different properties, separable
- **Meso = has stereocenters + internal mirror plane = achiral = optically INACTIVE**
- R/S: rank by atomic number, lowest priority away, 1→2→3 clockwise = R. **Fischer with H horizontal → reverse the answer**
- **(+)/(−) tells you nothing about R/S or D/L.** Racemic mixture = optically inactive
- **D-sugars, L-amino acids. All AAs are (S) except cysteine (R). Glycine is achiral**

**Reactions**
- **Reactivity ladder: acid halide > anhydride > ester ≈ acid > amide > carboxylate.** Downhill is free; uphill needs activation
- **β-lactam ring strain destroys amide resonance → abnormally reactive** (penicillin)
- **Aldehyde more reactive than ketone** (less steric, less electron donation)
- **NaBH₄: aldehydes and ketones only. LiAlH₄: everything.** Aldehyde→1° alcohol, ketone→2° alcohol
- **Acetal = protecting group** (inert to base/nucleophile/hydride, removed by aqueous acid). **A cyclic sugar is a hemiacetal; a glycosidic bond is an acetal**
- 1° alcohol --PCC--> aldehyde; --CrO₃/KMnO₄--> carboxylic acid; 2°→ketone; **3° → no reaction**
- **α-H pKa ~20** (enolate resonance); **β-dicarbonyl ~10**. **Anything α to a carbonyl racemizes** via the planar enol
- **Retro-aldol cleaves the Cα–Cβ bond** — this is the aldolase step of glycolysis
- **Kinetic enolate = less substituted = LDA, cold.** Thermodynamic = more substituted = weak base, warm
- **Decarboxylation requires a carbonyl at the β position** (β-keto acids, malonic acids)
- **Saponification is irreversible; Fischer esterification is reversible**
- **Tosylate formation retains configuration** (C–O bond never breaks); the following SN2 inverts

**Biological**
- **His pKa 6.0 — the only side chain buffering at physiological pH**
- pI = average of the two pKa's flanking the neutral species. Acidic AA → average the two lowest; basic AA → the two highest
- **Peptide bond is a planar, non-rotating, trans amide** (~40% double-bond character) — this is why secondary structure exists
- **Cys + Cys --oxidation--> disulfide.** Reduced by DTT/BME
- **Anomeric carbon = the ring carbon bonded to two oxygens.** α = OH down, β = OH up (Haworth, D-sugar)
- **Sucrose is NOT a reducing sugar** (both anomeric carbons locked). Everything else common is
- Glucose/galactose = C4 epimers; glucose/mannose = C2 epimers
- **Cis double bond kinks the chain → lower MP → more membrane fluidity.** Trans behaves like saturated
- **Hydroquinone ⇌ quinone = 2e⁻/2H⁺ redox** = ubiquinone in the ETC
- Purines = **A, G** (bicyclic). Pyrimidines = **C, U, T** (single ring)
